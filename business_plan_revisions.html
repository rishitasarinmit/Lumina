Orientation

How to read this

Each step section follows the same order: the original assumption or plan, what the synthetic study showed, the recommended revision, a confidence level, and how to validate it with real people. Counts are out of 10 personas unless stated. Text in quotation marks is copied verbatim from the synthetic transcripts. Persona IDs: P1 Raj (Innovator patient), P2 Dr. Okafor (CMIO, champion), P3 Maria (caregiver), P4 Karen Mitchell, RN (RPM nurse manager), P5 Denise (transplant patient), P6 Linda (spouse caregiver), P7 Dr. Brennan (colorectal chief), P8 Greg (CFO), P9 Hal (rural patient), P10 Frank (rejector).

High

Consistent across at least 7 of 10 personas and present in both segments (patients/caregivers and clinical/buyers). These drive the plan.

Medium

A clear pattern within one segment, or 4–6 personas across both, or a direct inference from High findings. Act on it, but test early.

Low

Needs data. Based on 3 or fewer personas, synthetic numbers (for example n=3 price points), or analyst reasoning. Treat as a placeholder.

Change types: Revise change the original plan · Keep leave as is or defer · New did not exist in the plan · Test a real-world experiment. Step numbers follow Aulet's book (2013). Earlier project notes numbered some steps one higher (for example "Step 9" for Quantify the Value Proposition); the step name is authoritative.

DE Steps 3 and 8 · Headline rewrite

Before and after

Value proposition

Before (original thesis)

Post-surgical patients wear a ring 24/7 for 14 days. It builds a digital twin of their personal baseline, and RPM nurses act on baseline-deviation alerts. Health systems pay through CMS RPM codes to avoid 30-day readmission penalties, and patients and caregivers welcome passive tracking.

After (recommended)

For health systems carrying the cost of surgical episodes under CMS TEAM or other risk contracts, Sepsis Sentinel is a surgeon-prescribed, vendor-staffed 24/7 triage service for the first 14 days at home. A comfortable, edema-tolerant wearable collects signals against a pre-op baseline and the expected post-op trajectory. Our nurses screen every alert overnight, call the patient or caregiver with a clear next step, and forward only explained, pre-triaged escalations into Epic. The system pays per episode, with an option to tie fees to documented episode savings, and adds no net work for its own nurses. Patients and caregivers get a named person who knows them and answers at 2 a.m.

End user profile statement

Before (Stage 1 persona file)

"Adults aged 60+ and/or immunocompromised who have been discharged home within 14 days after inpatient surgery (colorectal, cardiac, orthopedic joint, transplant, abdominal), plus the family caregivers who manage their recovery, plus the hospital clinical and economic stakeholders (RPM nurses, surgeons, CMIO/digital health leaders, CFOs) who would deploy and pay for monitoring."

After (recommended)

Adults recovering at home in the 14 days after an elective TEAM episode surgery (starting with major bowel) at a TEAM hospital with an RPM hub. The end user is a triad: the wearer, often older, on opioids and with swollen or arthritic hands; the family caregiver who operates the device and takes the calls; and the RPM or triage nurse who acts on alerts. All three face the same night-time question of whether a sub-101°F temperature and feeling "off" is normal recovery or the start of sepsis, and none of them has anyone to ask before morning.

DE Step 1

Market Segmentation

ReviseMedium

Original assumption / plan

Segment by patient: adults 60+ or immunocompromised, after any inpatient surgery (colorectal, cardiac, orthopedic, transplant, abdominal), with health systems as a single buyer type paying through RPM codes.

What the synthetic study showed

No persona named CMS RPM codes as the primary funding source (0/10); 3 buyers called RPM a partial offset (P2, P4, P8). The buyers who gave a funding source pointed to TEAM/episode or ACO budgets (P2, P8) or a service-line budget (P4). Under fee-for-service, an avoided readmission can cost revenue.

“For commercial fee-for-service, avoiding a readmission can actually cost us revenue.”

P8, CFO

Recommended revision

Put the buyer's risk position first in the segmentation. Primary axis: who pays for a post-discharge complication (TEAM episode participant, ACO or integrated-plan lives, Medicare Advantage risk, pure fee-for-service). Secondary axes: surgery type (elective vs emergency, since elective allows a pre-op baseline), existence of an RPM hub, and patient hand and connectivity constraints. Fee-for-service-only systems become a low-priority segment.

Validate with real humans by

Interviewing 8–10 real CFOs and revenue-cycle leads across TEAM, ACO and fee-for-service systems about who absorbs the cost of a post-op readmission. Confidence is Medium because only 4 synthetic buyers spoke to it.

DE Step 2

Beachhead Market

ReviseMedium

Original assumption / plan

Any US health system with post-surgical patients at risk of readmission, motivated by readmission penalties.

What the synthetic study showed

Three personas noted post-op sepsis is not an HRRP penalty condition (P4, P7, P8). The CFO said the money lands only in episode or ACO budgets. All 3 frontline clinicians (P2, P4, P7) told a recent bowel-surgery story where a patient was "off" for 1–2 days, or data sat unwatched over a weekend, before presenting septic.

“Honestly, TEAM is the only place it clearly lands.”

P8, CFO

Recommended revision

Beachhead: hospitals selected for CMS TEAM (mandatory from January 2026, with episode budgets that include 30 days after discharge) that already run an RPM or virtual care hub. Start with one episode type. Major bowel is the lead candidate because it matches the leak window and the clinical stories; lower-extremity joint replacement is the alternate. CABG, spinal fusion and hip/femur fracture follow within TEAM. Verify which hospitals and episodes are in TEAM from current CMS lists.

Validate with real humans by

Pulling each target hospital's own TEAM episode volume and 30-day readmission causes for major bowel versus joint replacement, and asking 5 real service-line leaders which episode is over target and why.

DE Step 3

End User Profile

ReviseHigh

Original assumption / plan

A single end user, the patient, who wears the ring and welcomes passive tracking because of anxiety about complications.

What the synthetic study showed

  • All 10 personas raised ring fit (swelling, arthritis, sizing); 9/10 raised charging or connectivity.
  • 9/10 described the core ambiguity of telling normal recovery from early complication. All 6 patients/caregivers had a sub-101°F low-grade temperature episode; in all 4 night-time episodes (P1, P6, P9, P10) nobody called before morning.
  • 8/10 asked for an accountable human owner or interpreter when asked what one thing they would change.
  • Caregivers operate the device and take the calls (P3, P6, and P9's wife June).

“they're going to be the real operators for a lot of these patients”

P2, CMIO, on caregivers

Recommended revision

Replace the single patient with a triad: (a) the wearer, older, often on opioids, with swollen or arthritic hands; (b) the family caregiver who charges, fits and receives calls; (c) the RPM or triage nurse who consumes alerts. Narrow to elective TEAM episodes so a pre-op baseline is possible. Immunocompromised patients (P5) have high need because fever is blunted. Design alternatives for emergency surgery (no baseline, P9), rheumatoid or severe arthritis (P9, P10) and homes without cellular coverage.

Confidence split: the triad, hand constraints and night-time ambiguity are High. Narrowing to elective TEAM episodes is Medium.

Validate with real humans by

Observing 15–20 real discharges and follow-up home visits for one TEAM episode type, and measuring finger circumference on post-op days 0, 3, 7 and 14. Interview the caregivers separately from the patients.

DE Step 4

TAM for the Beachhead

ReviseLow

Original assumption / plan

TAM sized from all 60+ post-surgical discharges times RPM reimbursement per patient.

What the synthetic study showed

The RPM revenue line is doubtful for a 14-day episode (P4, P7, P8), the buyer is the episode budget holder, and patients will not pay (3/6 refuse any out-of-pocket cost). So both the unit count and the price per unit in the original TAM are wrong.

“RPM billing is not a clean fit for a fourteen-day episode.”

P2, CMIO

Recommended revision

Rebuild the beachhead TAM bottom-up. This page adds no new market-size figures; every input below is to be validated.

Beachhead TAM / yr = [# TEAM hospitals with an RPM hub] × [eligible major bowel episodes per hospital / yr] × [share clinically eligible for home monitoring] × [per-episode price, to be validated]

Replace the RPM-reimbursement price with the per-episode service price from Step 16. Size RPM billing separately as a possible offset, and only after compliance review.

Validate with real humans by

Sourcing hospital and episode counts from current CMS TEAM participant files, and checking eligibility share with 3 real surgical service lines. Low confidence until then.

DE Step 9

Identify Your Next 10 Customers

NewMedium

Original assumption / plan

Not defined. The plan assumed demand from any hospital with an RPM program.

What the synthetic study showed

Buyers asked for their own data before any money moves: a retrospective on their own TEAM readmissions (P8) and a pilot on their own patients with a comparison group (P2, P4, P8). The C2 referrals named APPs, after-hours line nurses, transplant coordinators, revenue cycle, the CISO and risk management.

Recommended revision

Recruit 10 real customers that match the persona, each with the other DMU roles reachable:

  • 4 RPM or virtual-care nurse managers at TEAM hospitals with a major bowel volume, hub closed nights or weekends.
  • 2 at systems with an integrated health plan or ACO lives (Okafor-type).
  • 2 at community systems with thin margins (Halvorsen-type).
  • 1 at an orthopedic-heavy TEAM hospital (joint replacement alternate).
  • 1 at a transplant center, to test the immunocompromised sub-segment.

For each, record: hub hours, nurse FTEs, current alert noise, TEAM episode volume, who covers nights, and the name of the colorectal chief and CFO.

Validate with real humans by

Counting how many of the 10 agree to a second meeting that includes their CFO or surgeon. Fewer than 3 would signal the persona or beachhead is wrong.

Full Life Cycle Use Case

ReviseHigh

Original assumption / plan

Ring issued at discharge, worn 24/7 for 14 days, learns the baseline after discharge, and alerts go to "the care team".

What the synthetic study showed

8/10 raised that no one owns the alert, especially at night; every hub described is daytime-only. The Innovator lost about 4 days of data right after surgery. Three personas asked for structured human touchpoints (P4, P7, P10).

“a notification at 2 a.m. goes to nobody in my world”

P4, RPM nurse manager

“So I've got a gap of about four days in my data, right when you'd most want it.”

P1, patient

Recommended revision: post-op day 0 to 14

Filled markers are overnight. The overnight owner is the vendor's 24/7 triage nurse, who escalates to the hospital's on-call surgery team under a surgeon-signed standing protocol.

  1. Pre-op visit
    Surgeon's office enrolls through an Epic orderThe surgeon or their nurse explains it in the patient's language. The messenger is the patient's own clinician.
  2. Day −14 to −1
    Sizing kit and pre-op baselineFitted at pre-admission testing; 7–14 days of baseline for elective cases.
  3. Day 0
    Removed for the OR; refit at dischargeLarger size, adjustable ring or non-ring form factor if hands are swollen. Cellular hub, no phone pairing. Teach-back with the caregiver.
  4. Days 0–3
    72-hour window: trajectory modeCompare against the pre-op baseline and the expected post-op trajectory, since post-op physiology is already abnormal. Non-wear or low battery triggers a caregiver nudge, never a clinical alert.
  5. Day 2
    Human touchpoint 1Nurse call, replacing or absorbing the existing 48-hour call.
  6. Any night
    Tiered escalation, owned by vendor triage nurseNurse call, then photo or recheck, then same-day visit or CRP, then ED with call-ahead. Epic In Basket receives only pre-triaged escalations.
  7. Day 5
    Human touchpoint 2Covers the leak and SSI window that opens days 5–10.
  8. Day 9
    Human touchpoint 3Last structured call before the clinic visit.
  9. Day 14
    Close-outPrepaid return mailer, data deletion confirmation, outcome report to the service line and finance.

The need for a named overnight owner is High (8/10, both segments). The exact call days (2, 5, 9) are Medium, drawn from 3 personas.

Validate with real humans by

Walking the timeline with 3 real hub teams and 2 on-call surgery teams, then running it as a concierge pilot (Step 21, test 5). Record where hand-offs fail, especially between 9 p.m. and 7 a.m.

DE Step 8

Quantify the Value Proposition

ReviseHigh

Original assumption / plan

Value = avoided HRRP readmission penalties plus RPM revenue (99453 99454 99457 99458).

What the synthetic study showed

A3 was invalidated as stated: 0 Validated, 6 Partial, 4 Invalidated. No persona called RPM the primary funding. Buyers price in labor and alert volume.

“A cheap device that doubles my alert volume is the most expensive thing you could sell me.”

P4

“So an alerting tool can actually make my numbers worse in exactly the contracts where I'm supposed to be saving money”

P8

Recommended revision

Stop leading with HRRP. Sepsis is not an HRRP target condition (HRRP covers AMI, heart failure, pneumonia, COPD, CABG and THA/TKA, with a penalty cap of 3% of base DRG payments). Express value in the buyer's currency. The structure below uses placeholders; it reports no results.

Net value per 100 episodes = [avoided readmissions per 100] × [cost per readmission inside TEAM episode] + [avoided ED visits per 100] × [cost per ED visit] + [displaced nurse-hours] × [loaded hourly cost] + [displaced call-vendor spend] − [false-alarm ED visits and CTs] × [cost each] − [residual health-system nurse minutes] × [cost per minute] − 100 × [per-episode price] + [RPM offset, only if compliant]

Operating metrics to publish alongside: forwarded alerts per 100 patient-days, PPV, lead time in hours versus current detection, and caregiver sleep and anxiety. Buyer thresholds stated by synthetic personas, to be confirmed: forwarded false positives under one-third (P4); payback within 12 months and at least 1.5x return (P8). P8 also sketched a break-even of about $350K per year at $200 per episode for about 1,100 episodes plus one triage FTE, needing roughly 15–25 avoided readmissions. That is a synthetic persona's estimate, not a validated figure.

For caregivers, the value line is “Just knowing that if something happens at 2 a.m., somebody knows before 7 a.m.” (P3).

High for moving off HRRP and RPM as the lead (0/10 validated A3, both segments). Medium for TEAM as the replacement currency (2–3 buyers).

Validate with real humans by

Filling every placeholder with a real CFO's own TEAM data (Step 21, test 4). Confirm current RPM code rules with revenue cycle: 99454 historically required at least 16 days of data per 30 days, and the CY2026 Physician Fee Schedule added shorter options (2–15 days of device supply and a 10-minute management code). Verify current CMS rules and payer policy, including whether RPM can be billed inside a surgical global period.

DE Step 10

Define Your Core

NewMedium

Original assumption / plan

Implied core: the ring hardware and its personal-baseline algorithm.

What the synthetic study showed

The single biggest unmet need (8/10) is an accountable person who owns the patient overnight. The champion framed alert thresholds as a staffing decision.

“It also taught me that the threshold isn't a technical decision, it's a staffing decision.”

P2

“That's not a device, that's just, somebody paying attention.”

P10

Recommended revision

Define the core as overnight clinical ownership at low alert volume: the combination of (1) a surgeon-signed tiered protocol library by procedure, (2) a pooled 24/7 triage staffing model with known minutes per alert, and (3) post-op-trajectory-adjusted alert logic tuned on outcome data. Hardware can be sourced or swapped. Protocol, staffing data and outcome-tuned thresholds accumulate with every episode and are hard to copy.

Validate with real humans by

Checking in the Wizard-of-Oz triage test whether real nurses achieve lower minutes per alert with the protocol than without it. If sensors from any vendor perform the same, that confirms hardware is not the core.

Recommended revision

A paid, time-boxed concierge service for one TEAM episode type (major bowel) at one hospital:
