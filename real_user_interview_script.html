Sepsis Sentinel Interview Guide

A ready-to-use guide for real interviews with post-surgical patients, family caregivers, clinicians and health-system buyers. It revises the v1 script using what the synthetic study of 10 personas surfaced. Every question keeps a problem-first, open-ended form.

Methodological disclaimer

Synthetic user research provides a rapid, low-cost hypothesis test, but IT CANNOT RELIABLY PREDICT REAL HUMAN BEHAVIOR. All findings, objections, and willingness-to-pay metrics generated by synthetic personas must be validated through primary market research with real patients, clinicians, and health system buyers.

The shaded "What the synthetic study suggested" boxes below are hypotheses to test. They are for the interviewer only. Never read them aloud, show them, or use their wording in probes.

Before you start

How to use this guide

Run each interview in order: warm-up, problem discovery, the concept statement, value and commitment, then close. Pick the Patient/Caregiver or Clinician/Buyer line for each question. Caregivers answer about the person they care for; substitute that person's name.

Consent, IRB and privacy

  • Get informed consent before starting and again before recording. Recently discharged patients may be tired or on opioids; offer to reschedule or split the session.
  • Ask your IRB or institutional research office whether the study needs review or an exemption determination, especially if you recruit through a health system.
  • Collect no PHI beyond what the analysis needs. Record a participant code, segment, procedure category and post-discharge day. Do not record names, dates of birth, MRNs, addresses or exact dates in notes.
  • If a health system recruits for you, work under its privacy office rules (HIPAA, and a BAA if you handle its patient data). Store recordings encrypted and delete them on the schedule in your consent form.
  • Offer compensation that your IRB approves, and pay the same amount whatever the participant says.

Length and recording

  • Plan 45 minutes: warm-up 4, problem discovery 22, concept 10, value and commitment 7, close 2.
  • Record audio only, with explicit permission. If the participant declines, take written notes and write up within one hour.
  • Use two people when you can: one asks, one takes notes on the template at the end of this guide.
  • Buyer interviews: book 45 minutes and prioritize Q8, Q11 and Q12 if time is cut.
  • Interview patients in their home or by phone or video. Patients with no smartphone are a required part of the sample; offer a landline call.

Clinical safety protocol

You are not the participant's clinician. If a patient or caregiver describes symptoms happening now that worry them or you (fever, chills, confusion, fast breathing, a wound that is red or draining, feeling much worse), pause the interview. Encourage them to contact their surgical team or after-hours line now, or call 911 in an emergency. Do not give medical advice, and do not resume unless they want to.

Background for buyer interviews (verify before relying on it)

Know these so you can follow the participant, never to lead them. Verify current CMS rules and payer policy with a revenue-cycle expert.

  • Sepsis is not a Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program (HRRP) target condition. HRRP covers AMI, heart failure, pneumonia, COPD, CABG and THA/TKA, with a penalty cap of 3% of base DRG payments.
  • RPM codes: 99453 99454 99457 99458. Device-supply code 99454 historically required at least 16 days of data per 30 days. The CY2026 Physician Fee Schedule added shorter-duration options (2–15 days of device supply, and a 10-minute management code).
  • Billing RPM inside a surgical global period is an open compliance question.
  • CMS TEAM (Transforming Episode Accountability Model) started in January 2026 as a mandatory episode model at selected hospitals for select surgical episodes, including LEJR, CABG, major bowel, spinal fusion and hip/femur fracture. Episode budgets include 30 days after discharge.

Discipline

Interviewer rules

  1. No solution before Section C. Never say "ring", "wearable", "sensor", "digital twin", "monitoring", "alert" or "sepsis" before the concept statement. Say "complications" or "getting sick" unless the participant raises infection first.
  2. Past behavior over hypotheticals. Ask "Tell me about the last time…" and "What did you do?", not "Would you…?"
  3. No yes/no questions. If one slips out, follow it with "Tell me more about that."
  4. No leading. Do not suggest answers, examples or emotions. Do not reuse the participant's words to steer them. Never repeat phrases from the synthetic callouts.
  5. Probe the story. "Tell me more." "What happened next?" "Why?" "Who else was involved?" "How did that turn out?"
  6. Hold a 5-second silence after each answer before you move on. People often add the most useful detail there.
  7. Note who raises what unprompted. Mark any mention of nights, who answers, hands, charging, cost or privacy that came before you asked.
  8. Read the concept statement verbatim, once. Do not explain it, defend it or add features. Answer factual questions with "What would you expect?"

Script v1 → v2

What changed from the v1 script

  • Removed priming. v1 Q2 introduced the word "off", which then recurred in answers. v2 Q3 asks about a moment the participant "wasn't sure" instead. v1 Q1 said "day and night"; v2 Q1 drops it so nights can come up unprompted.
  • Added three problem questions: the overnight hours (Q2), who participants want to hear from (Q4), and concrete past experience with things worn on the hands after surgery (Q5).
  • Merged v1 Q3 (tracking tools) into Q4 probes, and v1 Q9 and Q10 (first reaction and day-and-night fit) into Q10.
  • Buyer variants now probe episode-based budgets and who staffs calls and alerts after hours (Q8), and who would see a notification at which hours (Q11).
  • Concept statement: the v1 wording is kept as Statement A for comparability. An optional Statement B changes only the form factor, to test whether objections are about fingers or about the idea.
  • Added a forced-choice exercise (E1) to test the synthetic finding that people want a person more than a device, and a behavioral commitment close (Q12b) coded on a five-step ladder, alongside Van Westendorp.
  • Added assumptions A5–A8 that the synthesis surfaced, and tagged every question with the assumptions it tests.

The script

Interview script v2

Bold lines are what you say. Patient/Caregiver lines sit on a blue label, Clinician/Buyer lines on a grey label and in italics. Probes are neutral follow-ups; use as many as the time allows.

What the synthetic study suggested

Rapport answers surfaced the binding constraints early. Caregivers described exhaustion (P3 not sleeping great, P6 about 4 hours a night). Buyers described capacity limits (P2 30+ meetings a week, P4's hub open 8–5 weekdays, P8 a 1.2% margin).

Synthetic personas recited their persona-sheet traits almost verbatim. Real people will be less articulate and less on-message. Note whether real caregivers raise sleep loss and buyers raise capacity without prompting.

Q1The first days at homeA4
Patient / Caregiver

Clinician / Buyer

What the synthetic study suggested

Nights dominated the first two weeks for all 6 patients/caregivers. Clinicians and buyers described the time between the 48–72h call and the 10–14 day visit as a gap: P2 After that call, it's a black hole. and P8 silence until something shows up.

Still needs real-world validation

  • Synthetic patients were interviewed on days 3–6, so days 7–14, the classic leak window, were never observed. Interview at least half of real patients on days 7–14, or use a 14-day diary.
  • Record whether nights come up unprompted now that the question no longer names them.
Q2The overnight hoursA4A5New
Patient / Caregiver

Tell me about the nights since you came home. Think of the last night when your health or recovery was on your mind. What happened, and what did you do?

Clinician / Buyer

Walk me through what happens today when a recently discharged surgical patient has a concern overnight or on a weekend. Tell me about the last time it happened.

Probes

  • What time was it? Who else was awake?
  • Who, if anyone, did you contact? When?
  • How did you decide between contacting someone and waiting?
  • C/BWho hears about it first? How long until someone who can act does?
  • C/BWhat did that cost your team the next day?
vs v1New question. Nights were the strongest recurring theme but v1 only reached them through Q1.

What the synthetic study suggested

In all 4 night-time episodes (P1, P6, P9, P10) nobody called until morning, or at all. "2 a.m. / 3 a.m." recurred across 8 personas. P1 and P10: didn't want to be the guy who calls. Clinicians: P7 Night is where it dies., P4 a notification at 2 a.m. goes to nobody in my world.

Still needs real-world validation

  • Whether real patients actually wait until morning, and why (social cost, distance, not knowing). Recall is tidier than behavior; pair with a diary or after-hours call logs.
  • Whether real hubs and surgical offices truly have no overnight coverage, or whether an answering service or house supervisor absorbs these calls.
  • LLM homogeneity: the shared "2 a.m." motif may be model style, not shared vernacular.

Clinician / Buyer

The strongest evidence in the study: 6/6 patients/caregivers had a low-grade temperature episode (99.x to 100.2°F) and none met the call-above-101°F rule. Section C · Neutral concept exposure

~10 min · read verbatim, once

Use Statement A by default. For alternate interviews within each segment, use Statement B, which changes only the form factor. Note the version on the template. Do not add who answers, what it costs or what it detects. Record whether the participant asks "who" on their own.

Statement A · default (v1 wording, unchanged)

"Some teams are exploring a small ring worn on the finger that passively measures heart rate, temperature, and similar signals around the clock for about two weeks after discharge. It learns a person's own normal pattern and can notify a care team if the pattern changes. It is an early idea, and I'm looking for honest reactions, including reasons it would not work."

Statement B · optional, form-factor-neutral

"Some teams are exploring a small sensor worn on the finger, wrist or chest that passively measures heart rate, temperature, and similar signals around the clock for about two weeks after discharge. It learns a person's own normal pattern and can notify a care team if the pattern changes. It is an early idea, and I'm looking for honest reactions, including reasons it would not work."

First reactions split 2 positive (P1, P3: Honestly?   2. The sensor just described, with a nurse reviewing its readings around the clock.

Kill criteria

Red flags that would invalidate each assumption

Tally these across real interviews, by segment. One red flag is an anecdote; the same red flag from most of the decisive segment is a verdict.

A1 Adherence

  • Most patients describe taking off jewelry, watches or clips in the first 72 hours after discharge.
  • Caregivers say no one in the home would charge or check a device.
  • Patients recall abandoning prior home devices within days, not weeks.
  • Clinicians report their own home-device programs losing most patients within two weeks.

A2 Workflow & alert fatigue

  • RPM nurses say any new alert stream in their queue is a non-starter, even pre-screened.
  • Nurses report disengaging from past tools after a handful of false alerts.
  • Surgeons say they would push to switch off anything that pages overnight.
  • Per-alert documentation is required and nobody has time for it.

A3 Economic alignment

  • No buyer can name a budget line or a past program that was funded this way.
  • Revenue cycle says RPM cannot be billed for these patients (global period, data-day rules).
  • Finance leaders say avoided readmissions do not change their numbers.
  • Patients refuse any cost-sharing, including coinsurance.

A4 Patient pull

  • Patients who described night worries still say they would rather wait until morning.
  • Most first reactions frame the concept as surveillance or burden.
  • In E1, most pick nurse calls even when the sensor comes with a nurse.
  • Patients say they would wear it only to please their surgeon.

A5 Overnight ownership

  • No party (hospital, surgical group or vendor) is willing to own alerts at night.
  • Buyers will not pay for staffed triage inside the price band they give.
  • After-hours line nurses say they cannot absorb added calls.

A6 Ring on post-op hands

  • Most patients describe finger swelling or arthritis that makes any finger item impossible during days 0–7.
  • Clinicians say rings must come off for IV access, procedures or imaging.
  • Statement B draws no more acceptance than A, which suggests the objection is to wearing anything.

A7 TEAM as payer path

  • Finance leaders at TEAM hospitals cannot direct episode dollars to a vendor before savings are proven.
  • They accept only pure risk-share with nothing upfront, at a size that cannot fund 24/7 staffing.
  • TEAM volumes at target hospitals are too small to matter.

A8 Signal separation

  • Interviews cannot invalidate this. Flag it when clinicians describe post-op physiology so variable that a personal baseline is meaningless, then route to the shadow-mode study.
  • Clinicians or patients describe known accuracy problems on darker skin or with the medications common in the beachhead.
