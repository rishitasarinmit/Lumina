## Section A: Warm-Up

**Interviewer:** Thank you for making time, Linda. To start, tell me a little about yourself and what life looks like for you right now.

**Linda:** Well, I'm seventy-two, I was an accountant for about forty years, and I've been retired a while now. Right now life is mostly David. He came home from the hospital three days ago after his heart surgery, and it's just the two of us in the house. Our daughter is up in Seattle, so she calls every night, but day to day it's me. I have to say I'm a little tired. I have arthritis in my hands and knees, and I'm not sleeping much. But we're managing. I don't want to make it sound worse than it is.

**Interviewer:** When you say you're not sleeping much, tell me more.

**Linda:** I wake up and listen to him breathe. He has COPD, so his breathing is never quiet, and now I can't tell what's normal and what isn't. Maybe four hours a night, in pieces. I lie there and count, which is silly, I know.

**Interviewer:** How did the surgery come about, and how have things gone since coming home?

**Linda:** It was fast. He had a stress test that came back positive, and within about a week they told us he needed a triple bypass. There wasn't much time to think about it. The hospital was good, but the surgeon was very busy. He was kind, but you could tell he had somewhere to be. They sent us home with a binder, a thick one, and I've read it three times now. Since we got home it's been okay, I think. He's tired and sore and grumpy. He doesn't like me fussing over him.

---

## Section B: Problem Discovery

**Interviewer:** Walk me through the first two weeks after leaving the hospital. What stands out, day and night?

**Linda:** Well, it has only been three days, so I can only tell you about those. The medications, first of all. He's on eleven now. Some are new, some doses changed, and I made a chart on paper and it's on the refrigerator. In the morning it's pills, then his weight and blood pressure, then the breathing exerciser, then checking the incisions, the one on his chest and the one on his leg where they took the vein. I also have to call about cardiac rehab, and I haven't gotten through yet. At night it's worse, because during the day at least I'm doing things. At night I'm just lying there worrying.

**Interviewer:** What's the worry at night about?

**Linda:** That I'll miss something. The binder has a list of warning signs, and I counted once, it's about twenty things. Fever, redness, swelling, shortness of breath, chest pain that's different from incision pain, dizziness, weight gain. How do I know which one matters? He's always a little short of breath. His hands shake from the Parkinson's. So half the list, he already has a little bit of. I'm not a nurse. If he gets worse and I didn't catch it, that's on me.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about a time since he came home when something felt "off." What did you notice, what did you do, and how did it turn out?

**Linda:** The second night. He felt warm to me, and he was breathing faster, I thought. I took his temperature and it was 99.4. The binder says call if it's over 100.4, I think, so it wasn't that, but I didn't like it. I sat up with him for about an hour and took it again, and it was 99.1. I didn't call anyone because it was two in the morning and I didn't want to bother them for nothing. In the morning I called the advice nurse and she said that's common after surgery and to keep watching. So it turned out fine. But I didn't go back to sleep.

**Interviewer:** What went through your mind during that hour?

**Linda:** Whether I was overreacting or underreacting. That's the whole problem, really. I don't know which one I'm doing. The nurse was nice, but "keep watching" is what I was already doing.

**Interviewer:** What are you doing today to keep track of how recovery is going? Walk me through the tools, routines, or people involved.

**Linda:** I have the checklist on the fridge, and I write the numbers in a notebook. Weight every morning, blood pressure twice a day, temperature, and his oxygen. We already had a pulse oximeter from his COPD, the little clip on the finger, and a blood pressure cuff and a scale. Plus the breathing thing, the spirometer. For people, it's the advice nurse line, which I've called three times already, and our daughter every night. She asks me for the numbers, and I read them to her. Honestly, I like writing them down. Forty years of ledgers, I suppose. If it's on paper I know it's done.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about your daughter's part in that.

**Linda:** She worries, and she's far away, so she does what she can. She sends me articles, a lot of them. Gadgets, watches, apps for seniors. I know she means well. But every time one comes through I feel like she thinks I'm not doing enough. I'm doing everything in the binder. I don't need more things to read.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about a health device, app, or routine you or David were asked to use at home and stopped using, or never started. What happened?

**Linda:** The breathing exerciser, the spirometer. He's supposed to do it ten times an hour when he's awake, and he does maybe a few times a day, and only if I stand there. He says "stop fussing, Linda." And a few years ago, after the COPD diagnosis, they wanted us to use an app to log his symptoms. I set it up with the nurse and we used it maybe a week. It kept asking the same questions, and I was already writing things down anyway, so I stopped. Nobody ever asked me about it again, so I guess it didn't matter. I also have the health system's website with the messages, the portal, but I usually just call. I'd rather talk to a person.

**Interviewer:** Why did it stop with the app, do you think?

**Linda:** It was double work. And I didn't know who was looking at it, if anyone. If nobody's reading it, why am I typing it in?

**Interviewer:** Describe what David's body, especially his hands, arms, and sleep, has been like since surgery. What has he been able or unable to wear or keep on comfortably?

**Linda:** His chest is very sore, so he sleeps propped up in the recliner, not in bed. His leg is swollen where they took the vein, and his ankles too. His hands, well, the tremor is there, it's been getting a little worse, and his hands seem puffy to me since the surgery. He's supposed to wear compression stockings and he hates them. I have to put them on him, and with my arthritis that's a fight for both of us. He used to wear a watch and he stopped last year because the clasp was too hard with his hands shaking. He pulls at the oximeter clip too, when I put it on. He doesn't like things on him.

**Interviewer:** And your own hands?

**Linda:** Not great. Mornings are bad. The pill bottles, the stockings. I manage. I don't want to make a thing of it.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the last time a reading, warning, or alarm turned out to be nothing. How did you feel, and what did you do the next time it went off?

**Linda:** The oximeter. Yesterday it read 86, and his normal with the COPD is about 92 or 93. My heart just dropped. I was reaching for the phone to call 911. Then I thought, wait, his hands are cold and they're shaking, so I warmed his hand and tried the other finger, and it said 93. I still called the advice nurse, and she said the clip can be wrong if the hand is cold or moving. I felt foolish, and also shaky for the rest of the afternoon. Now I take it three times and write down the middle one. But I don't fully trust it anymore, and if it said 86 again, I'd still panic first. I can't help it.

**Interviewer:** What happened next that day?

**Linda:** Nothing, really. He was fine. He was annoyed with me for fussing. I sat in the kitchen for a while.

**Interviewer:** How do you usually decide whether a health product or service is worth paying for, and who normally pays?

**Linda:** Medicare pays, and we have a supplemental plan. If the doctor orders it, it's usually covered, and if it's covered I don't really think about it. If it's something extra, I'm pretty careful. We're on a fixed income, it's comfortable, but I don't buy things because an article says to. The oximeter we bought at the drugstore because the lung doctor told us to, and it was maybe forty dollars. If the doctor tells me we need it, I'll get it. If my daughter tells me, I'll think about it. If the television tells me, no.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about that difference.

**Linda:** The doctor knows David. The advertisements want my money. And my daughter, she means well, but she's not the one who has to set it up.

**Interviewer:** If you could change one thing about those first two weeks after discharge, what would it be, and why that?

**Linda:** I'd want someone to tell me what actually matters. Not twenty things, three or four. "Linda, if you see this, call. If you see that, don't worry." And maybe a nurse who calls me, the same one, every day or two, so I'm not always the one deciding whether to bother someone. I don't want more numbers. I have plenty of numbers. I want someone to carry part of it. That's the one thing.

**Interviewer:** Why a person, specifically?

**Linda:** Because a person can hear how he sounds, and hear how I sound. And I can ask a question back.

## Section C: Neutral Concept Exposure

**Interviewer:** I'm going to read a short description now, just once. "Some teams are exploring a small ring worn on the finger that passively measures heart rate, temperature, and similar signals around the clock for about two weeks after discharge. It learns a person's own normal pattern and can notify a care team if the pattern changes. It is an early idea, and I'm looking for honest reactions, including reasons it would not work."

**Interviewer:** What's your first reaction? What, if anything, concerns you about it?

**Linda:** Hmm. (pause) I suppose my first thought is, that's another thing. I know that sounds negative. The part about it going to the care team, not to me, I like that better than the apps my daughter sends. But a ring on David? His fingers are swollen, and he won't keep a watch on, and he pulls off the oximeter. And I'd worry about the computer deciding something is wrong. If it tells the nurse something is wrong in the middle of the night, then what? Does someone call me at three in the morning? That would frighten me half to death. And if it's wrong, like the oximeter was, then I've had a terrible night for nothing.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about the computer deciding.

**Linda:** I just don't feel comfortable with a machine judging whether he's sick. A nurse, yes. The doctor, yes. A computer that "learns his normal," I don't know what that means. His normal is being short of breath and shaky. How would it know the difference? I'm not saying it can't. I'm saying I wouldn't know whether to believe it.

**Interviewer:** Walk me through how something like this would fit, or not fit, into a normal day and night for you over those two weeks.

**Linda:** Well, I'd be the one putting it on him and making sure it stays on, because he won't care about it. Does it need charging? Then that's one more thing on my list, and I'd forget, or I'd be up at night worried that I forgot. Does it go on the phone? I use my iPhone for calls and texts and WeChat, and that's about it. If it needs an app and passwords and Bluetooth, I'd have to wait for my daughter to walk me through it on the phone, and it would take an evening. And his hand is swollen, so what if it's too tight, or it comes off in the recliner and I'm crawling around looking for it with my knees? The good version, I suppose, is if it's just on him and I don't have to do anything, and someone else watches it. Then maybe I'd sleep a little. But I'd have to see that to believe it.

**Interviewer:** What would happen on the first night, do you think?

**Linda:** Honestly, I'd probably check on it more, not less. At first anyway. I'd lie there wondering if it was working.

**Interviewer:** What would need to be true for you to trust a notification from something like this? What would make you stop paying attention to it?

**Linda:** It would have to come from his doctor's office, from a nurse who knows him, not a text from a machine. And the nurse would need to tell me exactly what to do. "Take his temperature, call us back in an hour," or "take him in now." Clear steps. If I got a scary message and nobody told me what to do, I'd call 911, I know I would. As for stopping, if it went off and it was nothing, once, I'd probably still believe the next one, because I'd be too scared not to. Twice or three times, I think I'd start to hate it, and David would take it off and tell me it's a toy. He'd win that argument if it had been wrong. And if nobody ever called me at all, I wouldn't know if it was working or if they were just not looking, like the app.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about David's side.

**Linda:** He doesn't want to be a patient. He wants to sit in his chair and watch the news and be left alone. If a doctor tells him to wear it, he'll grumble but he might. If I tell him, or our daughter, forget it.

## Section D: Value & Willingness to Pay

**Interviewer:** How would you expect something like this to be paid for? For the two-week period: at what price would it seem so cheap you'd doubt it works, a good deal, getting expensive, and too expensive to consider?

**Linda:** I'd expect Medicare or the health plan to pay, if the doctor orders it. If it's part of his care, it should be covered like the rest. If they told me it's not covered and I have to pay myself, I'd be pretty hesitant, to be honest, and I'd ask the nurse whether we really need it. Prices are hard for me to guess because I don't know what these things cost. (pause) All right, if you want numbers. If it were ten dollars or less, I'd think it's a toy. Twenty-five, maybe thirty for the two weeks would seem fair if the doctor said we should. Around seventy-five I'd start thinking hard about it and probably ask my daughter. At a hundred and fifty, no, I wouldn't do it out of pocket, not for something I'm not sure about. We already have the oximeter and the thermometer.

**Interviewer:** And if the doctor said it was important?

**Linda:** Then I'd probably pay the seventy-five, grudgingly. I'd do what the doctor says. But I'd wonder why Medicare wouldn't pay for it if it was so important.

## Section E: Close

**Interviewer:** Is there anything I should have asked but didn't?

**Linda:** You asked a lot about David, which is fine, but nobody asks about the person at home. I'm seventy-two with bad knees, and I'm doing the nursing now. Whatever you make, if it gives me more to do, I won't use it, or I'll use it and be more tired. If it takes something off my plate, that's different. Also, and I don't mean this rudely, I'd rather have a nurse call me every other day than have a ring. If you can do both, fine, but the nurse is the part I'd want.

**Interviewer:** Who else should I talk to about this?

**Linda:** Our daughter, I suppose, she'd be very interested, she likes these things. But talk to the nurses on the advice line too, because they're the ones who'd get the calls. And there's a women's group at our church, several of them have been through this with their husbands. Some of the ladies in my WeChat group too, though a lot of them would want it explained in Chinese. We all have the same binder, more or less.

**Interviewer:** Thank you, Linda. This was very helpful.

**Linda:** I hope I wasn't too negative. I'm just tired. Good luck with it.
