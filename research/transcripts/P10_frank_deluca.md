## Section A — Warm-Up

**Interviewer (W1):** To start, tell me a little about yourself and what life looks like for you right now.

**Frank:** What life looks like right now? It looks like my living room ceiling, pal. I'm 69, retired IBEW, I pulled wire in half the buildings between Broad Street and the river for thirty-some years. Divorced, live by myself in the same rowhome I've had since '88. Right now I'm sittin' here with my leg up on this ice contraption, and the walker's parked next to me like a nurse that don't talk. My sister comes by every other day to make sure I ain't dead. That's about it.

**Interviewer:** What does a normal day look like when you're not recovering?

**Frank:** Normally I'm out. Coffee with the guys at the union hall a couple mornings, I help out on side jobs when somebody's kid needs a panel looked at, and I fish when I can get up to the Poconos. I haven't been up there in two years because of the knees. That's the whole point of this surgery, honestly. I want to stand in a creek again without feelin' like somebody's got a screwdriver in my joint.

**Interviewer (W2):** How did the surgery come about, and how have things gone since coming home?

**Frank:** Bone on bone, is what the doc said. I'd been gettin' cortisone shots for years, and they quit working. So they did the replacement at the orthopedic place, the specialty hospital. And I gotta tell you, it's a factory. In the morning, out the next day. Post-op day one they had me up with the walker, did two laps around the hallway, and some lady's handing me a folder, "You're good to go, Mr. DeLuca." Good to go where? I live alone. I got fourteen steps to my bedroom.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about that discharge.

**Frank:** I asked about going to a rehab place for a week, a short stay. My plan said no. Medicare Advantage, through the union retiree plan. Denied. They said I didn't "meet criteria." Criteria. Some computer looked at my file and decided I'm fine. I'm 69 with RA and a new knee and I live by myself, but I'm fine. So I'm sleepin' on the couch downstairs because I ain't doing those steps yet.

## Section B — Problem Discovery

**Interviewer (Q1):** Walk me through the first two weeks after leaving the hospital. You're about five days in. What stands out so far, day and night?

**Frank:** The nights are the worst. Pain wakes me up around two, three in the morning, and there's nobody to talk to, so I just lay there and watch YouTube videos of guys fixing old boats. During the day it's PT three times a week, which is a whole production because I can't drive, so it's my sister or a ride from one of the guys. The swelling is something else. The knee's like a cantaloupe. And the quiet. I'm not gonna sit here and cry about it, but you notice how quiet a house is when you can't get out of it.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned the quiet. Can you say more about that?

**Frank:** When I was working, I was never home. After the divorce it was fine, I had the hall, I had the guys, I had things to do. Now I'm stuck on a couch and the phone don't ring as much as you'd think. My sister's good, but she's got her own life, her husband's not well either. So it's me, the TV, and the ice machine humming. I'm not lonely, I'm just... bored out of my skull. Put it that way.

**Interviewer (Q2):** Tell me about a time during recovery when something felt "off." What did you notice, what did you do, and how did it turn out?

**Frank:** Two nights ago. The skin around the incision got real red, like pink going on red, and it felt hot to me. I don't know what's normal for a new knee. Nobody tells you. The folder says "call if you notice redness," but it's a knee that just got sawed open, of course it's red. So I took my temperature. 99-point-something. I figured that's nothing. I went to sleep.

**Interviewer:** What went through your mind that night?

**Frank:** Honestly? Infection. My buddy Sal, he had a hip done maybe six years ago and it got infected, and they had to go back in and take the whole thing out, put in a spacer thing, he was on IV antibiotics for weeks. He's never been right since. So yeah, I thought about Sal. And I'm on methotrexate for the RA, they held it for the surgery, but the doc told me I'm more prone to infections with that stuff. So it's in the back of my head. I just didn't want to be the guy who calls at midnight over nothing.

**Interviewer:** What happened next?

**Frank:** Next morning I called the office. Got a nurse, she asked me a bunch of questions, told me if it spreads or I get a fever over 101 or it starts draining, go to the ER. So I drew a line around the red with a Sharpie, like my old man used to do. It hasn't gone past the line. That's my system. And look, if I go to the ER every time something's pink, I'm sittin' there eight hours at Jefferson with a bunch of people coughing on me, and they're gonna run every test known to man to cover their own behinds. That's defensive medicine. I'm not doing that unless I have to.

**Interviewer (Q3):** What are you doing today to keep track of how recovery is going? Walk me through the tools, routines, or people involved.

**Frank:** Thermometer, the regular kind, from the drugstore. I take it when I feel hot, not on a schedule. The ice machine, that I use a lot. I had the CPM machine, the one that bends your leg for you, I sent that back after three days, I couldn't stand it. The walker. The Sharpie line. And my sister, every other day, she looks at it and tells me I look like hell, which is her way of saying she loves me. And the PT kid, he looks at it too, three times a week. That's the tracking. Me.

**Interviewer:** Why did you send the CPM machine back?

**Frank:** It's this big thing, you strap your leg in and it cranks your knee back and forth for hours. I couldn't sleep in it, couldn't get up to use the bathroom without unstrapping everything, and I'm fumbling with the straps with these hands. The PT said I'm doing fine without it anyway, so I called the company and they came and got it. One less thing.

**Interviewer (Q4):** Tell me about a health device, app, or routine you were asked to use at home and stopped using, or never started. What happened?

**Frank:** You want the list? CPAP. They said I had sleep apnea, gave me the mask, I wore it maybe three weeks. It's like sleeping with a vacuum cleaner on your face. And then I find out the machine's sending my usage to the company, and if I don't use it enough the insurance stops paying for it. So it's spying on me in my own bed. It's in the closet. Blood pressure cuff, the doc wants me to check it every day and write it down. I check it... sometimes. When I remember. Maybe once a month.

**Interviewer:** You said the CPAP was sending your usage. How did you find that out?

**Frank:** The supply company called me and said "we see you haven't been compliant." Compliant. Like I'm on parole. I didn't sign up for somebody watching me sleep. Nobody told me it had a modem in it. That was the end of that, I unplugged it that day.

**Interviewer (Q5):** Describe what your body, especially your hands, arms, and sleep, has been like since surgery. What have you been able or unable to wear or keep on comfortably?

**Frank:** The hands are bad right now. They held the methotrexate for the surgery, so the RA is flaring. My knuckles are swollen up like walnuts, stiff in the morning, I can barely do a button. I can't wear my watch because my wrist's puffy, it's on the kitchen counter. I haven't worn my wedding ring in... it's gotta be ten years, since before the divorce even, because it wouldn't go over the knuckle. Sleep, I told you, two, three hours at a stretch, on the couch, with the knee up. Anything tight on me I take off. Even the compression stockings they gave me, I'm supposed to wear them, I wear one out of two, some days.

**Interviewer:** What happens with the compression stockings?

**Frank:** You try pulling a compression stocking on with RA hands and a knee that don't bend. It's a twenty-minute wrestling match. My sister helps when she's here. When she's not, it don't happen. I'm being honest with you.

**Interviewer (Q6):** Tell me about the last time a reading, warning, or alarm turned out to be nothing. How did you feel, and what did you do the next time it went off?

**Frank:** The ice machine, actually, it's got a little alarm when the water gets low, and one night it went off three times and the water was fine. Some sensor thing. I pulled the plug on the alarm part. And the car, my check-engine light, it's been on for a year, the mechanic says it's the gas cap sensor. You learn which lights mean something. I spent my whole life around electrical stuff; sensors lie. A false alarm, the first time you check. The second time you grumble. Third time, it's in the drawer.

**Interviewer:** How did it feel that night with the ice machine?

**Frank:** Annoyed. It's two in the morning, I finally fall asleep, and the thing's beeping at me over nothing. I'm up, I'm hobbling around with the walker to check the water, it's full. I said, "Told you so," to nobody. Then I was up the rest of the night. So that's what a false alarm costs me: a night of sleep I don't get back.

**Interviewer (Q7):** How do you usually decide whether a health product or service is worth paying for, and who normally pays?

**Frank:** I don't pay. That's how I decide. I paid union dues for forty years so I'd have a plan that covers me, and if the plan don't cover it, I don't get it, unless it's life or death. The thermometer was nine bucks, fine. I'm on a pension and Social Security, I'm not poor, but I'm not throwing money at gadgets. And when the plan does pay for something, I start wondering why. Nobody gives you nothing for free. If the insurance company is paying for something, it's because it saves them money, and usually that means it saves them money on me.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about that. What do you mean "saves them money on me"?

**Frank:** The rehab denial. I read about it in the union newsletter and heard it on the radio too: these Medicare Advantage outfits use a computer program to decide how many days you get in rehab, and it cuts people off. There was a whole lawsuit about it. So I got denied, and I'm like, I bet that's what happened to me. Nobody looked at me. A computer did. So when somebody offers me something "to help," I want to know who's getting what out of it.

**Interviewer (Q8):** If you could change one thing about those first two weeks after discharge, what would it be, and why that?

**Frank:** Keep me more than a day. That's number one. But if you're asking realistic... a person. A real person who knows my knee, who I could call at 11 at night and say "it's red, is this normal," and they'd say "send me a picture" or "I'll come by," and they wouldn't make me feel like an idiot. Not a call center. Not a menu, "press 2 for your knee." Somebody who knows my name.

**Interviewer:** Why a person, specifically?

**Frank:** Because I can size up a person. I can tell if they know what they're talking about. That nurse on the phone, she was okay, but she was reading off a card. I want somebody who's seen a thousand knees and can say "Frank, that's fine" or "Frank, get in here." I'd listen to that. I'd actually listen, if I trusted 'em.

## Section C — Neutral Concept Exposure

**Interviewer (read verbatim):** "Some teams are exploring a small ring worn on the finger that passively measures heart rate, temperature, and similar signals around the clock for about two weeks after discharge. It learns a person's own normal pattern and can notify a care team if the pattern changes. It is an early idea, and I'm looking for honest reactions, including reasons it would not work."

**Interviewer (Q9):** What's your first reaction? What, if anything, concerns you about it?

**Frank:** *(laughs)* A ring. You were listening to me, right? I can't get my wedding ring past my knuckle and you want to put a ring on me. Where's it go, my pinky? My pinky's swollen too. That's the first thing. The second thing is, it's a tracking device. You can call it whatever you want. It's on my body 24 hours, it knows when I'm sleeping, when my heart's going, where I am probably. Who's getting that? The hospital? The insurance company? Some company in California? Because I'll tell you what they'll do with it: they'll use it to say I'm fine when I'm not, or say I didn't wear it so they don't have to pay for something.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about the insurance piece.

**Frank:** Same as the CPAP. "You weren't compliant." Or, say this thing says my heart rate's normal and my temperature's normal, and I say my knee hurts and I need more PT, and they go, "Well, the ring says you're doing great, request denied." That's what I see. You're building them a better tool to deny people. Maybe you don't mean to. But that's where it goes.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything about it that doesn't concern you?

**Frank:** *(pause)* Look. I'm not gonna lie to you. The other night with the red knee, I thought about Sal. If something could've told me "that's normal, go back to sleep" or "this ain't right, call somebody," I would've slept better. That part, I get. The part where it catches an infection before it's too late, with the methotrexate and everything, I'm not stupid, I see why somebody would want that. It's the everything-else part I don't like. And the ring part. The ring part is a nonstarter for my hands.

**Interviewer (Q10):** Walk me through how something like this would fit, or not fit, into a normal day and night for you over those two weeks.

**Frank:** It wouldn't. Morning, my hands are at their worst, swollen, stiff. Anything on my finger is gonna be squeezing. I'd take it off by the time I had my coffee. Showering, I'm sittin' on a shower chair with one hand holding the grab bar, I'm not gonna be thinking about a ring. Night, I'm on the couch waking up every couple hours, if it's bugging my finger at 3 a.m. it's coming off and going on the end table, and then I'll forget it for two days. And who charges it? Me? With these hands, finding a little charger? I'm telling you straight: with my history, if it went on at all, it's in the drawer by day three.

**Interviewer:** What if the swelling weren't an issue? Say it fit.

**Frank:** Then it's just the tracking thing. Maybe I'd wear it for the doc if he asked me personal, like face to face, "Frank, I want you to wear this for two weeks, I'm the one looking at it." Maybe. But it'd have to be him, not some outfit. And I'd want to know it's off and gone after two weeks, not collecting on me forever.

**Interviewer (Q11):** What would need to be true for you to trust a notification from something like this? What would make you stop paying attention to it?

**Frank:** For me to trust it: a real person calls me, somebody from my surgeon's office, who knows who I am, and says "Frank, your numbers look off, tell me how you feel." Not a text, not a robot call. And they don't just automatically tell me go to the ER. If every time it pings they tell me go to the ER, forget it, I'll stop answering. What'd make me stop paying attention? One false alarm. Maybe two, I'm feeling generous. The first time it drags me out at night for nothing, it's "told you so" and it goes in the drawer with the CPAP mask.

**Interviewer:** You said "not automatically the ER." What would you want instead?

**Frank:** Options. "Send us a picture." "We'll have somebody look at it tomorrow morning." "Come into the office." The ER is the last resort. Every time you go there, it's thousands of dollars, it's tests, and you sit there. I trust a doctor who says "let's watch it" more than one who says "go to the ER," because the second guy is just covering himself.

**Interviewer:** And on the data side, what would need to be true?

**Frank:** In writing, it never goes to the insurance company. Never gets sold. Only my surgeon's people see it, it's erased after. And honestly? I wouldn't believe it even if you wrote it down. Every app says they don't sell your stuff and then you read on Facebook they did. So you'd have to get it past my union guys first. If the benefits fund people or somebody I trust at the hall said "this is legit, the data stays put," then maybe I'd listen. Not you, no offense. You're selling it.

## Section D — Value & Willingness to Pay

**Interviewer (Q12):** How would you expect something like this to be paid for? For the two-week period: at what price would it seem so cheap you'd doubt it works, a good deal, getting expensive, and too expensive to consider?

**Frank:** I wouldn't pay for it. Zero. I'm not being cute, that's the answer. If the hospital wants me to wear it, they pay for it, or the insurance does, and then like I said, I want to know why they're paying.

**Interviewer:** Understood. If you had to put numbers on it anyway, is there a price that would feel suspiciously cheap, or one that would feel like too much?

**Frank:** Too cheap? Free is too cheap. If it's free, I'm the product. I know that much from YouTube. Too expensive to consider is anything that comes out of my pocket. Five bucks, fifty bucks, doesn't matter, I'm not buying a tracking device for myself. If you're asking what I think it's worth to the hospital, I don't know, that's their problem. I'd guess it's a lot less than a guy like Sal going back in for another surgery, but that ain't my money.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything that would change your mind about paying, or about wearing it?

**Frank:** Paying, no. Wearing, maybe, if three things happened. One, it's not a ring; something I don't have to jam over a swollen knuckle. Two, my own surgeon, face to face, asks me to, and it's his office that looks at it and calls me, a person. Three, I get it in writing, and somebody I trust confirms, that my insurance company never sees a byte of it and it gets wiped after. Even then, I'm not promising I'd keep it on. I'm telling you what it would take to get me to try it. If the insurance made it mandatory, I'd wear it and I'd be angry about it, and I'd probably find a way to "forget" it.

## Section E — Close

**Interviewer (C1):** Is there anything I should have asked but didn't?

**Frank:** Yeah. You should've asked what happens when a guy like me says no. Because there's a lot of us. My whole union hall is guys like me, bad knees, bad backs, bad hands, don't trust nobody. If your thing only works on people who love gadgets, you're gonna miss the guys who actually live alone and don't call anybody. That's who ends up like Sal. So figure out what you do for the guy who won't wear it. Maybe that's just a nurse who calls every couple days. I'd take that call.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about that call.

**Frank:** Somebody calls, day three, day five, day eight, whatever. "Hey Frank, how's the knee, what's your temp, send me a picture of the incision." Five minutes. I'd do that. I'd probably look forward to it, to be honest, sittin' here all day. That's not a device, that's just, somebody paying attention.

**Interviewer (C2):** Who else should I talk to about this?

**Frank:** Talk to the guys at the hall, the retirees, we got a whole Tuesday coffee group, half of them have had a knee or a hip done. Talk to the benefits fund people, they're the ones who'd know about the insurance side and whether this stuff gets used against us. Talk to my sister, she's the one driving me everywhere, she'll tell you what it's actually like. And talk to the PT kid, he sees more of my knee than the surgeon does. Don't just talk to doctors. Doctors saw me for fifteen minutes.

**Interviewer:** Thank you, Frank. I appreciate your honesty.

**Frank:** Yeah, well. You asked. Good luck with it. I mean that, sort of.
