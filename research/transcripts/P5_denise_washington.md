## Section A — Warm-Up

**Interviewer (W1):** To start, tell me a little about yourself and what life looks like for you right now.

**Denise:** Well, I'm Denise, I'm 58, and I live here in Detroit with my son, Marcus. I was a school administrator for a long time, middle school office, until my kidneys made me go out on disability. I had six years of dialysis, and then my sister gave me one of her kidneys in 2019, so that's my whole life now, honestly, taking care of that kidney. Right now I'm home recovering from a hernia surgery, so life is a lot of sitting, a lot of pills, and a lot of watching the clock. My son works nights, so during the day it's me, the Lord, and the TV.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about what "taking care of that kidney" looks like day to day.

**Denise:** It's a schedule. Tacrolimus twice a day, and you don't miss it, you don't take it late, you don't play with it. Mycophenolate, blood pressure pills, the whole pill organizer, the big one with the four compartments. Labs every couple weeks, more right now because of the surgery. My sister didn't give me that kidney for me to be careless with it.

**Interviewer (W2):** How did the surgery come about, and how have things gone since coming home?

**Denise:** I had a bulge by my old incision that kept getting bigger, and the general surgeon said it needed to be fixed before it got stuck, strangulated, whatever the word was. It was supposed to be routine, and they kept saying that, routine, routine. I was in the hospital four nights, and I've been home six days now. It's been okay, I guess, sore, tired, I'm moving slow. But I don't trust "okay" in my body anymore, because my body doesn't tell me the truth like other people's does.

**Interviewer:** What do you mean when you say your body doesn't tell you the truth?

**Denise:** The medicine that keeps me from rejecting the kidney also keeps my body from fighting like it should. So I can have an infection and not run a fever. Other people get 102 and they know they're sick. I might be 99-point-something and be in real trouble. So I second-guess everything, every chill, every time I'm more tired than yesterday.

---

## Section B — Problem Discovery

**Interviewer (Q1):** Walk me through the first two weeks after leaving the hospital. What stands out, day and night?

**Denise:** The first night home was the hardest, because in the hospital somebody's checking on you every four hours whether you like it or not, and then all of a sudden it's nobody. Marcus brought me home, got me settled, and then he had to go to work that night. So I was lying there listening to myself breathe. During the day I do my notebook, I take my temperature, my blood pressure, I weigh myself, and I write it all down. At night is when my mind goes, because the fatigue is there anyway from the surgery and I can't tell if it's normal tired or sick tired. We're only at day six, so I can't tell you the whole two weeks yet, but that's what it's been.

**Interviewer:** What happens at night when your mind goes like that?

**Denise:** I get up and take my temperature again. Sometimes twice. I pray. And sometimes I look at the transplant group on Facebook, which is good and bad, because somebody always has a story that scares you worse.

**Interviewer (Q2):** Tell me about a time during recovery when something felt "off." What did you notice, what did you do, and how did it turn out?

**Denise:** This time, day three, I had a chill in the afternoon, and my heart felt like it was going fast just sitting on the couch. My temperature was 99.4, which for most people is nothing, but my normal runs around 97.8, so to me that's a number. I called the transplant coordinator line, and Tasha, she's my coordinator, she called me back in about an hour. She had me recheck, asked about the incision, asked about pee, and said if it goes to 100 or I feel worse, come in. It came back down that evening, so it turned out to be nothing, or nothing I know of.

**Interviewer:** You said "this time." Has there been another time?

**Denise:** Yes. And that one didn't turn out fine. About three years ago I had a UTI, and I felt terrible, just wrong, and I didn't have a real fever. I went to the ER and I waited nine hours. Nine. I told them I'm a transplant patient, I'm on immunosuppressants, and I felt like they looked at me like I was just another Black woman complaining. Nobody believed me until my lactate came back high, and then all of a sudden everybody's running. It was urosepsis. I was in the hospital a week.

**Interviewer:** What happened next, after that experience?

**Denise:** After that I started the notebook for real, and I learned my numbers, so I could say "my normal is this, and this is not my normal." And I call my transplant team first now, always, before I go anywhere. The ER is the last place I want to be. I'll go if I have to, but I go ready to fight.

**Interviewer (Q3):** What are you doing today to keep track of how recovery is going? Walk me through the tools, routines, or people involved.

**Denise:** Every morning: weight, temperature, blood pressure, and I write it in a composition notebook. I use a digital thermometer from Walgreens and an arm cuff, and it has to go on my right arm because my left arm has my old fistula from dialysis. I check the incision in the bathroom mirror. I use MyChart to look at my labs and send messages, though the surgery team is slow on MyChart, the transplant team is better. And Tasha, the coordinator, she's the real tool, if I'm honest. Marcus checks on me when he gets home in the morning before he goes to sleep.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about the difference between the surgery team and the transplant team.

**Denise:** The transplant team has known me since 2019. They know my numbers and they know I'm not a complainer. The general surgeons, I met them for this one thing, and they're nice, but they're in and out. When I asked them about infection and my meds, the resident kind of said, "Well, your transplant team will manage that." So nobody really owns me right now, if that makes sense. Each one thinks the other one's watching.

**Interviewer (Q4):** Tell me about a health device, app, or routine you were asked to use at home and stopped using, or never started. What happened?

**Denise:** The breathing thing, the plastic one with the ball you suck on. The incentive spirometer. I did it in the hospital because the nurse stood there, and I did it maybe two days at home, and now it's sitting on the dresser. It hurts my belly to use it, and nobody's asking me about it. Before that, a while back, the clinic had me on some blood pressure app that was supposed to send my readings in automatically with a Bluetooth cuff. It kept losing the connection, and I never knew if anybody on the other end was actually looking at it. So I went back to the notebook. With pills I'm perfect, because pills are life or death. Gadgets, I'm honest, I'm hit or miss.

**Interviewer:** What would make the difference between hit and miss for you?

**Denise:** Whether I can tell it's doing something. If I send my numbers and nobody ever says a word, why am I doing it? The notebook at least I can hold up and show somebody.

**Interviewer (Q5):** Describe what your body, especially your hands, arms, and sleep, has been like since surgery. What have you been able or unable to wear or keep on comfortably?

**Denise:** Sleep is bad. I'm a side sleeper and I can't turn over good with this incision, so I'm propped up on pillows and I wake up every couple hours. They sent me home with an abdominal binder, that big velcro belt, and I wear it in the day but at night it rides up and it's hot, so I take it off. My hands, the tacrolimus gives me a little shake, always has. And my fingers were puffy after surgery from all the IV fluid, so I took my rings off in the hospital and they're still in a dish on the dresser. My left arm I protect because of the fistula, nothing tight on that arm, no blood pressure, no blood draws, nothing.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about the swelling in your fingers.

**Denise:** It's better than it was, but it's not gone. My fingers are still a little tight in the morning. Some days my ankles swell too, and then I worry about the kidney, so that's another thing I write down.

**Interviewer (Q6):** Tell me about the last time a reading, warning, or alarm turned out to be nothing. How did you feel, and what did you do the next time it went off?

**Denise:** MyChart. Labs post to MyChart before the doctor even sees them, and last month my creatinine came up flagged high, with that little red marker. I didn't sleep that whole night. I thought, this is it, I'm losing her kidney. I called Tasha first thing in the morning and she said it was barely over, I was probably dehydrated, recheck next week. And it was fine next week. The next time something was flagged, I still worried, I'm not going to lie, but I waited for Tasha to call before I spun out. I don't think I'll ever not worry. When something says "abnormal" about my kidney or an infection, I'm going to call. I can't help it.

**Interviewer:** How did you feel about getting that result before anyone explained it?

**Denise:** Honestly, I'd rather know than not know. But a red flag with no explanation at eleven o'clock at night, with nobody to call until morning, that's cruel. That's the part I'd change.

**Interviewer (Q7):** How do you usually decide whether a health product or service is worth paying for, and who normally pays?

**Denise:** I don't pay. I can't. I have Medicare because of the disability, and Medicaid is my secondary, and between those two, if it's not covered, I'm not getting it, simple as that. I'm on a fixed income, and my anti-rejection medicines alone, if I had to pay for those I'd be dead. The thermometer and the cuff I bought myself because they're cheap and I needed them. If my transplant team says I need something, I ask them, "Is it covered?" If it's not, I ask them to find me a way, a program, a sample, something. I've seen too many people in my Facebook group get sold things, supplements and whatnot, that don't do anything.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about how you decide it's worth it, even when it's covered.

**Denise:** If Tasha or my nephrologist tells me I need it, I'll do it. If some ad on Facebook tells me, I won't. And it has to be worth the trouble, because I already have a lot of trouble. Every new thing is another thing to remember, another thing to charge, another phone call.

**Interviewer (Q8):** If you could change one thing about those first two weeks after discharge, what would it be, and why that?

**Denise:** That somebody believes me the first time. That's it. If I call and say I feel off, I don't want to have to prove it by getting sicker. I want the people deciding whether I'm sick to have something in front of them besides my word, because my word hasn't always been enough. And I'd want the surgery team and the transplant team talking to each other, so I'm not in the middle carrying messages. If I had to pick one, it's being believed.

**Interviewer:** Why that one over the others?

**Denise:** Because that's the one that almost killed me. Waiting nine hours with urosepsis wasn't a technology problem. It was a people problem.

## Section C — Neutral Concept Exposure

**Interviewer (reads verbatim):** "Some teams are exploring a small ring worn on the finger that passively measures heart rate, temperature, and similar signals around the clock for about two weeks after discharge. It learns a person's own normal pattern and can notify a care team if the pattern changes. It is an early idea, and I'm looking for honest reactions, including reasons it would not work."

**Interviewer (Q9):** What's your first reaction? What, if anything, concerns you about it?

**Denise:** Hm. My first reaction is, does it work on my skin? You know about the pulse oximeters, how they read Black patients' oxygen higher than it really was, and people got sent home during COVID who shouldn't have. I read about that, and our church did a whole health Sunday on it. So if this thing reads light through my finger, I want to know who they tested it on. The "learns my own normal" part, I'll be honest, that part I like, because that's what I'm doing with my notebook, my normal isn't the textbook's normal. But I have questions. Who sees it, where does the data go, and can it end up somewhere it hurts me.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about "somewhere it hurts me."

**Denise:** I'm on disability. I've had to prove I'm sick to the government, and I don't want some data showing I walked around a lot one week and somebody using that against me. Same with insurance. And I don't want a company selling my heart rate to whoever. If it's going to my transplant team and only to them, that's one thing. If it's going to a company in California, that's another thing.

**Interviewer:** Anything else that concerns you?

**Denise:** My medicines. Does it know I'm on tacrolimus and that I don't run a fever? Because if it's waiting for a fever the way the ER does, it's going to miss me the same way they did. And was it tested on transplant patients, or on young healthy people who wear these for their workouts? That's my question: was this tested on people who look like me and take my medications?

**Interviewer (Q10):** Walk me through how something like this would fit, or not fit, into a normal day and night for you over those two weeks.

**Denise:** Day, I'm home, I'm sitting, so wearing it wouldn't be the hard part in the day, probably. The problem is my fingers are still swollen, and I took my own rings off for that reason, so it would have to fit a puffy finger in the morning and not fall off by evening. And it can't go on my left hand, that's my fistula arm, so it would be my right hand, and I do everything with my right hand, washing, lotion, dishes. Charging, I don't know, how often? Because if it's dead and I don't notice, and it's supposed to be watching me at night, that's worse than not having it, because I'd think I'm covered. At night Marcus isn't here, so if something goes off at 3 a.m., who's getting that, and what are they doing about it?

**Interviewer:** What would happen next in that 3 a.m. situation, the way you picture it?

**Denise:** Honestly? I'd probably wake up anyway because I'm not sleeping. If they call me and say go to the ER, I have to call an Uber or wake up Marcus at work, and then I'm in the ER waiting room for eight hours again. So unless the ring changes what happens at the ER door, I'm not sure it changes much for me. Now, if it meant my transplant team calls ahead and says "she's coming, take her seriously," that's different. That would be worth something.

**Interviewer (Q11):** What would need to be true for you to trust a notification from something like this? What would make you stop paying attention to it?

**Denise:** Tasha would have to tell me it's good. Really, my transplant team would have to be the ones giving it to me and watching it, not some call center. I'd want to see that it was tested on Black patients and on transplant patients, real numbers, not a brochure with one Black lady smiling on it. And when it says something's wrong, I want a person to call me, and I want it to say what changed, like "your heart rate's been up for six hours," not just "alert." What would make me stop? If it goes off and it's nothing, a couple of times, I'd still call, because I can't not call. But I'd start to feel like it was crying wolf, and my coordinator would get tired of me calling about it, and then I'd feel like I was bothering people. I'd also stop if I found out it wasn't accurate on my skin. Then I'd put it in the dish with my other rings.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned wanting a person to call. Tell me more about that.

**Denise:** A beep on my phone at night with nobody behind it is just going to scare me, like that red flag in MyChart. I'd rather it go to somebody who knows me, and they decide if I need to hear about it. But then I also want to see my own numbers, because I don't want it to be something they know about me that I don't. Both. I know that's asking a lot.

## Section D — Value & Willingness to Pay

**Interviewer (Q12):** How would you expect something like this to be paid for? For the two-week period: at what price would it seem so cheap you'd doubt it works, a good deal, getting expensive, and too expensive to consider?

**Denise:** I would expect Medicare to pay for it, or the hospital, because if it keeps me out of the ICU they save a lot more than whatever this costs. I'm not paying for this out of my pocket. I'm going to be real with you, I'm not. I'm on a fixed income and I've got a copay for everything already.

**Interviewer:** I understand. If you imagine it had a price to you, even a hypothetical one, how would you think about those four points?

**Denise:** Okay, if you make me. Too cheap to work, I don't really think in those terms, because if my transplant team gives it to me free I'm going to assume it works, that's their job to check. If it's something off the internet for five dollars, then yeah, I'd think it's a toy. A good deal would be zero, covered, or maybe a ten-dollar copay like my other things. Getting expensive, twenty-five dollars for the two weeks, that's real money for me, that's groceries. Too expensive, anything over forty, fifty dollars, I'm not doing it, I'd go back to my notebook and my thermometer. And if it's a deposit, like I have to put down a hundred dollars and get it back when I return it, no, I can't float that either.

**Interviewer:** How did you arrive at those numbers?

**Denise:** That's just my life. I pay a few dollars here and there for copays and I watch every one of them. And I'm telling you that most of the people in my transplant group are in the same boat. If it's not covered, it's not happening, for most of us.

## Section E — Close

**Interviewer (C1):** Is there anything I should have asked but didn't?

**Denise:** You didn't ask me whether I'd rather have this or a nurse calling me every day for two weeks. I'd take the nurse. Maybe both, but if you made me pick, a person. You also didn't ask me who's going to explain it to me, set it up, show me how to charge it, because I'm going home from surgery tired and full of pain medicine, and they already hand you a stack of papers you can't read. And ask your people whether it works on dark skin, and whether it works on people whose immune system is turned down. If you can't answer those two, don't bring it to my community.

**Interviewer (C2):** Who else should I talk to about this?

**Denise:** Talk to the transplant coordinators, Tasha and them, because they're the ones who would actually get the calls and they know who never runs a fever. Talk to the transplant support group, the Facebook one, there's hundreds of us, and you'll get the real opinions, some people will love it and some people will tell you off. Talk to the National Kidney Foundation, they care a lot about this equity stuff. And talk to some ER triage nurses, because if they're not going to look at it, it doesn't do me any good. Maybe my pastor too, honestly. If you want Black folks in Detroit to trust something, the church is where you start.

**Interviewer:** Thank you, Denise. This was really helpful.

**Denise:** Mm-hm. Just make sure you listen to the parts you didn't want to hear.
