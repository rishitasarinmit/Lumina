## Section A — Warm-Up

**Interviewer (W1):** To start, tell me about your role and what a typical week looks like for you.

**Karen:** Sure. I run the remote patient monitoring program for our system, so I've got nine RNs and four MAs in a centralized hub, and we're open eight to five, Monday through Friday. We've got right around 400 patients enrolled, mostly CHF and hypertension, some COPD, on cellular blood pressure cuffs, scales, and a few pulse oximeters. A typical week for me is about half staffing and half putting out fires: somebody called out, a vendor dashboard is lagging, a physician didn't sign an order so we can't enroll. Then there's the billing piece, making sure every patient hits their data days and their twenty minutes so we can actually bill for the work we did. And honestly I still jump into the queue myself when we're short, which is more often than I'd like.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned the twenty minutes. Tell me more about that.

**Karen:** That's the interactive management time for the RPM management code. My nurses have to track time spent with the patient in the month, and document it, or it doesn't count. So on top of triaging readings, they're running a timer in their head on every call. It's one of those things that sounds small in a vendor demo and eats your whole Thursday afternoon in real life.

**Interviewer (W2):** Where do post-surgical patients fit into the work you and your team do?

**Karen:** Honestly? They mostly don't. Our program grew up on the medicine side, heart failure readmissions, hypertension control, so that's who we're built around. Surgical patients go home and they're in the surgeon's world, the clinic nurse makes a post-op call, maybe, and then we don't see them unless they show up in the ED. I'll be blunt, that's a gap. I was an ICU nurse for eighteen years before I hurt my back, and I've seen plenty of post-op patients come back through the door a lot sicker than when they left.

**Interviewer:** Why haven't they come into your program?

**Karen:** A few reasons. Nobody on the surgical side asked, for one. Surgeons don't really think about the hub; they don't return calls fast, and to be fair, they're in the OR. And our whole enrollment model is set up for chronic patients who stay on for months, not somebody who needs two weeks and then is done. The math and the workflow are just different.

## Section B — Problem Discovery

**Interviewer (Q1):** Walk me through what happens to a typical surgical patient between discharge and their first follow-up visit.

**Karen:** From what I see from the outside, and I want to be clear I'm not in that workflow every day, they get discharged with a stack of paper instructions: call if your temp is over 101, watch your incision, here's your pain meds. Somebody from the surgical clinic is supposed to make a post-discharge phone call within a couple of days, and whether that happens depends on the service and who's staffing that week. Then there's a follow-up visit at maybe ten days to two weeks. In between that it's basically the patient and whoever's at home deciding whether something is bad enough to call. And a lot of our older patients don't want to bother anybody, so they wait.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about the waiting.

**Karen:** It's a real thing, especially out here, a lot of our folks are rural and older. They'll tell you, "I didn't want to be a bother," or "I figured it was just part of healing." The daughter is usually the one who finally calls. By then you've lost a day, sometimes two. And after-hours, calls go to an answering service and then to whoever's on call for surgery, which is not always a fast turnaround.

**Interviewer (Q2):** Tell me about the most recent post-surgical patient who came back sicker than expected. How was it discovered, and what, if anything, could have happened differently?

**Karen:** There was one this spring that stuck with me, a man in his seventies, bowel resection, and he was actually one of our heart failure patients, so he was already on our cuff and scale. He went home on a Thursday. Over the weekend his heart rate was creeping up, low hundreds, and his blood pressure was drifting down, but it was the weekend and we're not open, so nobody was looking. Monday morning my nurse sees it in the queue, calls him, he's confused on the phone, and his wife says he hasn't eaten in two days. He went to the ED and was septic, anastomotic leak, ended up in the ICU.

**Interviewer:** What happened next?

**Karen:** He survived, thank God. But my nurse was really shaken, and so was I, because the data was sitting right there all weekend. We should have caught that. And then the second thought I had, which I'm not proud of, was "who's liable for data we collected and nobody looked at?" That's the part that keeps me up. Could it have been different? Sure, if somebody was watching on Saturday and had a clear protocol to call him. But I don't have anybody watching on Saturday, and I don't have the FTEs to put somebody there.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned liability. Tell me more.

**Karen:** Once you're collecting data, there's an expectation that you're acting on it. Risk management asked me about it afterwards, we went through it. Our patient agreement says it's not an emergency service and we're not monitored after hours, and we tell patients that at enrollment. But patients don't remember that. They think "the hospital is watching me," and that's a dangerous misunderstanding.

**Interviewer (Q3):** What does your organization currently do to keep track of surgical patients after they go home? Who does it, and with what tools?

**Karen:** For most surgical patients, it's the post-discharge phone call from the clinic, a nurse or sometimes an MA with a script, and it's documented in Epic. Some service lines, I think ortho, use an automated text-message check-in through a vendor, but I don't know how much anyone looks at the responses. Then there's the follow-up appointment. My team isn't involved at all unless the patient happens to already be in our program for something else, like that man I told you about. So it's phone, Epic, and hope, basically.

**Interviewer (Q4):** What have you seen happen with patients' use of home devices over time? What drives drop-off?

**Karen:** We lose about forty percent of people by week three, somewhere around there, in terms of meaningful use. The first week everybody's great, they're taking their blood pressure twice a day, and then life happens. They feel fine, so why bother. Or the cuff hurts, or it's on the kitchen table and they forget, or the cellular hub unplugs and they don't know. With some of our older patients, a grandchild moved it, or it's the wrong cuff size and nobody noticed. And then there's the ones who just don't want to be reminded that they're sick.

**Interviewer:** What does your team do when that happens?

**Karen:** My MAs work a non-transmission list every morning and call people. If the patient doesn't answer, we call the caregiver, the daughter, the son, whoever's listed. That's a huge chunk of our time, frankly, chasing data rather than acting on it. And if they don't hit their data days, we can't bill that device code for the month, so we did the work for free.

**Interviewer (Q5):** What physical or practical barriers do post-surgical patients face with anything they are asked to wear or operate at home?

**Karen:** I'm speaking more from my ICU and floor years here than from our program, but post-op patients are tired, sore, and on pain medicine. They're foggy. Abdominal patients can't bend easily, some can't lift anything, and cardiac patients have sternal precautions. They're swollen, IV sites, sometimes their hands and feet are puffy for days. Anything with a lot of steps, like pairing to an app or charging every night, is going to fall to the caregiver, and not everybody has one. Some of our patients don't have a smartphone, and cell coverage out in the county is spotty, which is why we use cellular devices and not Bluetooth-to-phone.

**Interviewer (Q6):** Tell me about the last time an alert system or dashboard produced a lot of warnings that turned out to be nothing. What happened to how your team used it?

**Karen:** That's every day, honestly. On our current dashboard I'd estimate about thirty-five percent of our alerts are noise: a blood pressure taken right after they walked in from the mailbox, a scale reading when they had their shoes and coat on, a cuff put on upside down. A couple of years ago we had a pulse ox vendor where it was worse than that, alerts constantly for readings that were just bad signal, cold fingers, whatever. My nurses were clicking through red flags as fast as they could, and I could see it in their faces that they'd stopped really looking. So I went to the medical director and we raised the thresholds, and for a couple of parameters we turned the alerts off completely and just reviewed trends weekly.

**Interviewer:** How did you feel about that decision?

**Karen:** Uneasy. I did it because I'd rather have my nurses look closely at ten real alerts than skim a hundred. But every time you raise a threshold you're accepting that you're going to miss somebody. That's the trade-off vendors never want to talk about; they show you sensitivity in a slide deck and never how many false alarms a nurse has to clear per shift. I'd want that number on anything before it came near my hub.

**Interviewer (Q7):** Walk me through how the last post-discharge or remote monitoring program was funded, approved, or cancelled. Who had to say yes, and what numbers mattered?

**Karen:** When I stood the program up in 2021, it went through our VP of Ambulatory, who owns the budget, and then finance had to sign off on a pro forma. The numbers that mattered were the RPM billing revenue, based on how many patients we'd enroll and whether they'd meet the device and time requirements, against the cost of staff and devices. Then readmissions for heart failure, because that's got a penalty attached. I brought the clinical case and the staffing model; I don't sign anything. Every FTE since then I've had to fight for with a spreadsheet showing it pays for itself.

**Interviewer:** Has anything been cancelled?

**Karen:** We ran a COPD pilot for about nine months and it got scaled way back. Enrollment was lower than projected, adherence was bad, so the billing didn't come through, and we couldn't show a readmission change in that small a group. Finance looked at it and said it wasn't paying for itself. That's the reality: if it doesn't bill or it doesn't move a penalty number, it's hard to keep it alive, no matter how good the nurses feel about it.

**Interviewer (Q8):** If you could change one thing about those first two weeks after discharge, what would it be, and why that?

**Karen:** For surgical patients specifically? I'd want someone to actually own them. Right now it's this handoff where the hospital's done, the surgeon's clinic sort of has them, and nobody's really watching. I don't necessarily mean more technology, I mean a clear owner and a clear protocol: this is who calls, when, and what triggers sending them in. Even a structured nurse call on day two, day five, and day nine would catch some of those patients. Honestly, if you gave me the staff, that's what I'd do first, before any device.

## Section C — Neutral Concept Exposure

**Interviewer (concept statement, read verbatim):** "Some teams are exploring a small ring worn on the finger that passively measures heart rate, temperature, and similar signals around the clock for about two weeks after discharge. It learns a person's own normal pattern and can notify a care team if the pattern changes. It is an early idea, and I'm looking for honest reactions, including reasons it would not work."

**Interviewer (Q9):** What's your first reaction? What, if anything, concerns you about it?

**Karen:** My first reaction is, who's the care team? Because if it's my hub, my first question is who covers nights, and the second is whether I have to document every one of these notifications. Around the clock monitoring sounds great until you realize the patient I told you about got sick on a Saturday night, and a notification at 2 a.m. goes to nobody in my world. And if it goes to nobody, now we've got the same liability problem I just described, just with more data. I'll give it this: the passive part is interesting, because not needing the patient to remember to do something addresses some of my drop-off problem. But "learns their own normal" makes me nervous with post-op patients, because they don't have a normal right after surgery; everybody's tachycardic and running a low-grade temp on day two.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about that last part.

**Karen:** If it's learning their normal after they go home, their normal is already abnormal. If it's learning it from before surgery, you'd have to get the ring on them a week or two ahead, and good luck with that for most of our scheduled cases, and it doesn't work at all for the urgent ones. So I'd want to know exactly what it's comparing against. I've seen shiny things come and go, and the ones that died were the ones that sounded smart but nobody could explain to a nurse at the bedside.

**Interviewer (Q10):** Walk me through how something like this would fit, or not fit, into a normal day and night for your patients and your team over those two weeks.

**Karen:** Okay, so, day one: who puts it on and sizes it? If it's on the floor at discharge, that's a floor nurse with twelve other things, and if it's shipped to the home, that's the caregiver, and now we're troubleshooting fit over the phone. Swollen fingers after surgery, fluid shifts, the ring fits on day one and is loose on day ten, or it's too tight on day three. Then charging: whatever it needs to be charged, somebody forgets, and my MAs are back on a non-transmission list. On my side during the day, it would be another dashboard to log into unless it's integrated with Epic, and I'm not taking another standalone dashboard, I'll just tell you that now. And at night, nothing happens, because we're closed, so the honest version is it's a daytime review of overnight data, and you have to be upfront with patients about that.

**Interviewer:** What would it do to caseload?

**Karen:** That depends completely on the alert volume and how many patients. Surgical volume in our system is big; if they wanted every colorectal and cardiac patient on this, that's a lot of new enrollments turning over every two weeks, which means enrollment, education, and discharge from the program constantly. That churn is work. Chronic patients, you enroll once. I'd want to see a staffing model: X patients per nurse, Y notifications per patient per day, Z minutes each. If nobody can give me that, I can't take it to my VP.

**Interviewer (Q11):** What would need to be true for you to trust a notification from something like this? What would make you stop paying attention to it?

**Karen:** For me to trust it, I'd want to see published data, not a vendor white paper, in post-surgical patients, not healthy volunteers, with how many notifications were real and how many weren't. I'd want it to tell my nurse why it's alerting: heart rate trending up over twelve hours, temp up, whatever, so she can make a judgment. The algorithm doesn't have a license; I do, and my nurses do, so we have to be able to explain why we acted or didn't. And I'd need a standing protocol signed off by the surgeons: at this level, nurse calls the patient; at this level, she calls the surgeon; at this level, it's an ED referral. Without that protocol, every alert becomes a phone call to a surgeon who won't call back.

**Interviewer:** And what would make you stop paying attention?

**Karen:** Easy. If more than, I don't know, a third of them are nothing, like our current noise level, my nurses will start clicking through them, and I'll go raise the thresholds, same as I did before. If it alerts because the ring came off or lost signal and it looks the same as a real alert, that's done. And if every notification needs a full note in Epic, that's going to crush us; documentation is half the job already. The thing that makes nurses tune out isn't one false alarm, it's the fiftieth one on a Tuesday.

## Section D — Value & Willingness to Pay

**Interviewer (Q12):** How would you expect something like this to be paid for: RPM billing, bundled/episode budget, per-patient fee, or something else? Per monitored patient episode, what price would feel too cheap to be credible, a good value, getting expensive, and a non-starter? What evidence would you need before any money moved?

**Karen:** First, I want to be clear I don't have budget authority; that's my VP of Ambulatory and finance, so anything I say is my gut, not a commitment. How it'd get paid for: our finance people will immediately ask whether it bills under RPM, and a two-week episode is a problem there, because the traditional device code needs sixteen days of data in a thirty-day period. I know CMS added a shorter-duration code for two to fifteen days this year, but I honestly haven't seen what it's reimbursing for us yet, and our revenue cycle folks are still working through it. You'd also still need the twenty-minute or the newer shorter management time documented, a physician order, and consent, and I'd want compliance to confirm how it works inside a surgical global period, because I don't know that off the top of my head. Realistically, I think it gets paid out of a service line or quality budget, cardiac or colorectal, if somebody can tie it to readmissions or ICU days, because sepsis on its own isn't one of the readmission penalty conditions.

**Interviewer:** Understood, and I appreciate the caveat. From your gut, per monitored patient episode, what would those four price points be?

**Karen:** Okay, gut only. Too cheap to be credible: under about fifty dollars an episode. At that price I'd assume it's a consumer ring with a dashboard bolted on, and somebody's going to hit me with hidden costs later. Good value: somewhere around a hundred to a hundred fifty, roughly the neighborhood of what I'd guess we'd recover in RPM billing for a short episode, so it doesn't lose money before you count my nurses' time. Getting expensive: around two fifty, because then it has to be clearly preventing readmissions or ICU stays to justify it, and I'd need finance on board. Non-starter: north of about four hundred per episode, unless it comes with the monitoring staff, meaning they cover nights, and then that's a different conversation.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about that last point.

**Karen:** If a vendor said, "we provide 24/7 nurse monitoring and only escalate to your team with a real problem during business hours, or to on-call surgery overnight with a clear protocol," that changes my math, because now it's not my FTEs. I'd still be skeptical, because I've seen outsourced monitoring that just forwards everything back to us. And the price doesn't matter if the cost is my nurses' time. A cheap device that doubles my alert volume is the most expensive thing you could sell me.

**Interviewer:** And what evidence would you need before any money moved?

**Karen:** A pilot, in our own patients, with a defined population, a hundred or so colorectal or cardiac patients, and a comparison group. I'd want to see adherence, how many actually kept it on through day fourteen, the false-alarm rate per patient-day, how many nurse minutes it took, and whether it caught anything earlier than we would have otherwise. And it has to talk to Epic. If you can show me that it didn't add work, or replaced something we already do like the post-discharge calls, that's what gets my VP's attention. Readmission numbers would be nice, but honestly a hundred patients isn't going to prove readmissions to finance.

**Karen:** You didn't ask about who handles the devices after. Returns, cleaning, lost rings, inventory. We lose a meaningful number of BP cuffs every year, people just don't send them back, and that's with a return box. With something that small, it's going in a drawer or down a drain. You also didn't ask me about the patients, like health literacy and consent, whether they'd even understand what it is and isn't doing. And I'd ask whoever's building this whether they've ever actually staffed a monitoring hub, because that shows up in the design every time.

**Interviewer (C2):** Who else should I talk to about this?

**Karen:** My VP of Ambulatory, because she's the one who decides, and finance, because she'll send you to them anyway. Revenue cycle or our RPM billing person, to tell you whether a two-week episode pays anything. On the clinical side, you'd need a colorectal or cardiac surgeon who's willing to sign standing orders, and the surgical clinic nurses who make the post-op calls today; they'll tell you what's actually happening that I can't see. And risk management, because they're going to ask about after-hours data before anybody else does. Talk to a couple of the RPM managers in the AAACN groups too; we all compare notes on vendors.

**Interviewer:** Thank you, Karen. This was very helpful.

**Karen:** Sure. I'll be honest, I'm not against it, the surgical gap is real. But show me the night coverage plan and the false-alarm numbers before you show me the ring.
