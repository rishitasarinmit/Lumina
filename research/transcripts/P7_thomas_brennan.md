## Section A: Warm-Up

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time, Dr. Brennan. To start, tell me about your role and what a typical week looks like for you.

**Dr. Brennan:** I'm chief of colorectal surgery here. I operate three days a week and I'm in clinic two, and somewhere in there I run a department, teach residents, and sit on the surgical quality committee. I do about 350 cases a year: cancers, diverticular disease, IBD, a fair number of reoperations other people sent me. My week has no slack in it. I should tell you up front that I have about twenty-five minutes, not thirty. I've got a committee meeting after this.

**Interviewer:** Understood, I'll keep us moving. Where do post-surgical patients fit into the work you and your team do?

**Dr. Brennan:** They *are* the work. The operation is maybe a third of it; the rest is getting them through the next thirty days without a complication. We run an ERAS program, so a colectomy that used to stay a week goes home on day two or three now. That's good medicine, and I'm not going to argue with the data on it. But it means the part of recovery where things go wrong has moved out of the hospital and into the patient's house. My APPs take most of the post-op calls. I get pulled in when something sounds wrong.

## Section B: Problem Discovery

**Interviewer:** Walk me through what happens to a typical surgical patient between discharge and their first follow-up visit.

**Dr. Brennan:** They leave with written discharge instructions: call if your temperature is over 101.5, if the pain gets worse instead of better, if you see drainage or redness at the wound, if you can't keep fluids down, if your ostomy stops putting out or puts out too much. One of my APPs calls them at 48 to 72 hours. That's a scripted call: how's the pain, are you eating, bowels moving, any fever. Then they come to clinic around two weeks. In between, it's on the patient or the family to call us. Most of them do fine and we never hear from them.

**Interviewer:** And the ones you do hear from?

**Dr. Brennan:** That's the problem. The gap between the 48-hour call and the two-week visit is exactly when leaks and deep wound infections show up. Classically days five through ten. So the patient passes the phone call, they sound fine, and then on day six they're "just not feeling right" and they sit on it because they don't want to bother anybody. Or the wife calls, the answering service pages a resident, and the resident has to make a judgment over the phone about someone they've never met.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the most recent post-surgical patient who came back sicker than expected. How was it discovered, and what, if anything, could have happened differently?

**Dr. Brennan:** About a month ago. Low anterior resection, early seventies, uneventful stay, went home day three. APP call on day two post-discharge was fine. On day seven his daughter brought him to an outside ED because he was confused and wasn't eating. He was tachycardic, white count up, CT showed a pelvic collection. Anastomotic leak. He got a drain, antibiotics, transferred back to us, and he's okay, but he spent two weeks in the hospital and he'll probably end up with a diverting ostomy longer than we planned.

**Interviewer:** What could have happened differently?

**Dr. Brennan:** Honestly, I'm not sure anything would have changed the leak. Leaks happen; the question is how early you catch it. Looking back, the daughter said he'd been "off" for a day or two. Less appetite, sleeping more. Would he have come in a day earlier if someone had asked him on day five? Maybe. A day earlier with a contained leak is a drain. A day later can be a laparotomy. But I'll be straight with you: most of the patients who say they feel "off" on day five are just post-op patients who feel off. That's the hard part.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about that.

**Dr. Brennan:** Everybody after a bowel resection is a little tachycardic some of the time, runs a low-grade temp, feels lousy, sleeps badly. That's normal inflammatory response to an operation. The leak patient looks like that too for a while, and then he doesn't. Separating the two over the phone is most of the art of it. When I'm worried I want a CRP and I want a CT. I don't want more vague symptoms.

**Interviewer:** What does your organization currently do to keep track of surgical patients after they go home? Who does it, and with what tools?

**Dr. Brennan:** What I described. The APP phone call, the discharge instructions, the clinic visit. Everything lives in Epic. We have a secure texting platform the team uses internally. We track outcomes through NSQIP, so we know our readmission and leak rates after the fact, and I review those every quarter. There's no real-time tracking of anybody at home, and I'll be honest, nobody's asked me for one. The system has a remote monitoring program for heart failure, I think, through population health. It doesn't touch my patients.

**Interviewer:** What have you seen happen with patients' use of home devices over time? What drives drop-off?

**Dr. Brennan:** I'll give you the simplest example. Incentive spirometry. It's a plastic tube; we teach them in the hospital; it prevents pneumonia. I walk into a room on post-op day one and it's sitting on the tray table still in the bag. And that's while nurses are reminding them. At home, forget it. We've asked patients to log their ostomy output, drain output, weights. Some do it beautifully. Those are usually the engineers and the anxious ones. The older patient who lives alone, the one most likely to get into trouble, is the one who doesn't. Drop-off is mostly because they feel better, or they feel worse and they're too tired, or nobody's looking at the data so they figure why bother.

**Interviewer:** You said "nobody's looking at the data." Say more.

**Dr. Brennan:** If a patient writes down drain output for a week and brings me a sheet at clinic, that's nice, but I'm reading it after the fact. Nobody was watching it on day six. Patients figure that out fast.

**Interviewer:** What physical or practical barriers do post-surgical patients face with anything they are asked to wear or operate at home?

**Dr. Brennan:** They're tired and they hurt. Many of them are elderly. A lot of my cancer patients got chemo before surgery and have neuropathy in their hands, so fine motor stuff is hard. They've got an incision, maybe an ostomy they're learning to manage, which is a lot on its own. They're on opioids for a few days, sometimes they're confused. Fluid shifts mean hands and feet swell. And a good fraction don't have reliable internet or a smartphone they're comfortable with. Anything that needs charging, pairing, or remembering is going to fail in some of them.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the last time an alert system or dashboard produced a lot of warnings that turned out to be nothing. What happened to how your team used it?

**Dr. Brennan:** The Epic sepsis model. When they turned it on here, it fired on half the floor. Every post-op patient with a heart rate of 105 and a temp of 38 lit up. The nurses were paging residents for patients who were one day out of surgery and doing exactly what post-op patients do. Within a few months everybody was clicking through it. My residents learned to acknowledge it without reading it, which is the worst possible outcome, because now the one real alert gets clicked through too.

**Interviewer:** What happened next?

**Dr. Brennan:** I pushed at the quality committee to get it suppressed or re-tuned for surgical patients in the first 48 hours. I think they changed some thresholds. I don't look at it. I stopped trusting it, and it's very hard to get that trust back once it's gone. That's the thing I'd want anybody building one of these to understand. You get one shot.

**Interviewer:** Walk me through how the last post-discharge or remote monitoring program was funded, approved, or cancelled. Who had to say yes, and what numbers mattered?

**Dr. Brennan:** I don't hold a budget, so I can only tell you what I've seen from the committee side. Somebody from population health or an outside vendor came to the surgical quality committee a couple of years ago with a post-discharge phone and text outreach program. Automated check-in texts. Finance cared about readmissions and whether it was billable. We cared about who'd answer the flags. It went to a pilot on another service line and I don't know that it went anywhere. I didn't hear about it again, which usually means it quietly died. The numbers that mattered to me were who reads it and what the false positive rate is. The numbers that mattered to finance, I'd guess, were cost per patient and readmission penalties. You should ask them.

**Interviewer:** If you could change one thing about those first two weeks after discharge, what would it be, and why that?

**Dr. Brennan:** I'd want a real clinical touchpoint around day five to seven. Not another scripted call at 48 hours when everyone's fine. Somebody who knows the patient, eyes on them or at least a real conversation, and a low bar to get a CRP drawn. That's when leaks declare themselves. If I had to pick one thing, it's a better-timed human check, and a clear way for families to know when "off" means come in. I've lost two patients in 25 years to leaks that showed up at home. Both times, looking back, there was a day where somebody could have caught it. That's the day I'd want to cover.

## Section C: Neutral Concept Exposure

**Interviewer:** I'm going to read a short description once, and then I'd like your reactions.

> "Some teams are exploring a small ring worn on the finger that passively measures heart rate, temperature, and similar signals around the clock for about two weeks after discharge. It learns a person's own normal pattern and can notify a care team if the pattern changes. It is an early idea, and I'm looking for honest reactions, including reasons it would not work."

**Interviewer:** What's your first reaction? What, if anything, concerns you about it?

**Dr. Brennan:** My first reaction is that it's a consumer gadget looking for a medical indication. My second is specificity. Every post-op patient has tachycardia and low-grade temps, so "the pattern changed" is going to be true for basically all of them. How do you tell normal post-op inflammation from a leak or an abdominal sepsis picture? That's the whole question, and a ring on the finger doesn't obviously answer it. I've watched the Epic model fail at exactly that problem with a lot more data than heart rate and skin temperature. And "learns a person's own normal": what's normal? They didn't wear it before surgery, so the baseline you're learning is already a post-op baseline.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about the baseline piece.

**Dr. Brennan:** If you put it on at discharge, you're learning the pattern of someone who's two days out from a bowel resection, on opioids, not sleeping. That's not their normal. Maybe you can get them wearing it before surgery. We do see some of them weeks in advance at pre-op. But that's another step and another thing to go wrong. I'd also worry about what finger temperature means in a vasoconstricted, dehydrated elderly patient. I'm not an engineer, but I'd want to see how that signal behaves in my population, not in healthy people who bought one to track their sleep.

**Interviewer:** Anything else that concerns you?

**Dr. Brennan:** Two things. Liability, first. If that device logs a heart rate trend on day five and nobody acted on it, and the patient leaks on day seven, that data is going to be Exhibit A. Right now I'm responsible for what I know. This makes me responsible for a data stream I didn't ask for. Second, what happens when it alerts? My APP gets a notification at 9 p.m., calls the patient, who says "I feel okay, a little tired." Now what? The safe answer is "go to the ED," and now we've got an ER visit and a CT for a patient who was fine. Multiply that across a service and you've created a lot of unnecessary scans and visits, and a lot of scared patients.

**Interviewer:** Walk me through how something like this would fit, or not fit, into a normal day and night for your patients and your team over those two weeks.

**Dr. Brennan:** For the patient, the ring part is probably the easy part, if it doesn't need much charging and doesn't hurt. Though I'll say it again: patients don't do their incentive spirometry, so I'm not assuming 24/7 anything for two weeks. The ones who'll wear it are the ones who are already engaged. Swollen hands in the first week, maybe they take it off. For my team, it doesn't fit at all today. My APPs are in clinic, on the floor, fielding calls. Nobody is sitting at a dashboard. I'm not going to have my residents or APPs watching another screen. If somebody else, a nursing team, the vendor, whoever, did the first-line watching and only sent us something that had already been triaged, that's a different conversation. But then I need to know who that person is and whether I trust their judgment.

**Interviewer:** And at night?

**Dr. Brennan:** Night is where it dies. If this pages my resident at 3 a.m. because somebody's heart rate went up when they got up to use the bathroom, I'll have it turned off within a month. I'm not exaggerating. I'd be the one pushing to turn it off.

**Interviewer:** What would need to be true for you to trust a notification from something like this? What would make you stop paying attention to it?

**Dr. Brennan:** Data. Real data, in patients like mine. I'd want a prospective study, ideally randomized, in colorectal or at least major abdominal surgery, showing it picked up leaks or serious infections earlier than we do now, with a false alarm rate my team can live with. Earlier by how much, and did it change anything: fewer reoperations, fewer ICU days, not just "we detected it 18 hours sooner on a graph." Published in a journal I read, *Diseases of the Colon & Rectum*, *JAMA Surgery*, or presented at ASCRS. Or somebody I respect in colorectal surgery telling me they've used it and it didn't bury their APPs. Short of that, I don't trust a startup's internal validation, and I don't trust an administrator telling me it works.

**Interviewer:** And what would make you stop paying attention?

**Dr. Brennan:** The first handful of false alarms. Especially at night, especially if they send a patient to the ED who didn't need to be there. Also if the alert just says "pattern changed" without telling me what changed and how much. Give me the numbers: heart rate trend, temperature trend, over how many hours. If it's a black box score, I'll treat it like the Epic model. And if it misses a leak that we catch the old way, that'll be the end of it too. People remember the misses.

## Section D: Value & Willingness to Pay

**Interviewer:** How would you expect something like this to be paid for: RPM billing, bundled/episode budget, per-patient fee, or something else? Per monitored patient episode, what price would feel too cheap to be credible, a good value, getting expensive, and a non-starter? What evidence would you need before any money moved?

**Dr. Brennan:** I'm the wrong person to price it. I don't hold a budget, and I'm not going to pull a number out of the air for you, because you'll put it on a slide and it won't mean anything. I assume somebody would try to bill it under remote monitoring codes. I don't know the details, but I know there are requirements about how many days of data you need, and I've just told you I don't believe patients will wear it every day. So I'd be skeptical that the billing model holds up. And sepsis after surgery isn't one of the conditions the hospital gets penalized for on readmissions, as far as I know, so the finance people may not see the savings the way you'd hope. Ask the CFO.

**Interviewer:** Understood. Even roughly, is there any way you think about cost?

**Dr. Brennan:** The way I think about it is this: the expensive part isn't the ring, it's what it triggers. Every false alarm is an APP's time, maybe an ED visit, maybe a CT. If it's cheap and generates a lot of scans, it's expensive. If it catches a leak a day earlier and saves one reoperation, that pays for a lot of rings. But I don't know which of those it is, and neither do you yet. That's why I'd want the study before anyone spends money. Not a pilot that measures satisfaction scores. A study that counts leaks caught, time to intervention, false alerts per patient, and downstream ED visits and imaging. Then bring it to the quality committee and I'll read it.

**Interviewer:** And if that evidence existed?

**Dr. Brennan:** Then I'd look at it. That's all I'd promise. I'd need to see it in patients who look like mine, and I'd want the monitoring burden to sit somewhere other than my APPs.

## Section E: Close

**Interviewer:** Is there anything I should have asked but didn't?

**Dr. Brennan:** You didn't ask me what I'd do with the alert. That's the question. Say it fires. Then what? Is there a protocol: call, then CRP, then CT, with thresholds? Or does it just land on somebody's phone and they're on their own? If you can't answer that, the device doesn't matter. Also, you didn't ask what I'd want it compared to. Compare it to a well-timed nurse call on day five and a CRP. If it can't beat that, I don't need it.

**Interviewer:** Who else should I talk to about this?

**Dr. Brennan:** My APPs. They take the calls and they'd take the alerts, so ask them, not me, what it would do to their day. The chair of our surgical quality committee, and our NSQIP surgical clinical reviewer, who knows our actual complication data. Risk management, on the liability question. And outside my shop, the ASCRS folks who do ERAS and outcomes research. If one of them would take it seriously enough to study it, that'd mean more to me than anything you could tell me. I have to go. Good luck with it.
