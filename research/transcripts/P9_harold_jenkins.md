## Section A — Warm-Up

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time, Mr. Jenkins. To start, tell me a little about yourself and what life looks like for you right now.

**Hal:** Not much to tell. Farmed out here fifty-some years, six hundred acres, corn and beans. My boy runs it now. Right now I'm mostly sitting in the recliner, which I don't care for.

**Interviewer:** What does a normal day look like when you're not recovering?

**Hal:** Up at five. Coffee shop in town about seven with the fellas. Then I go out and bother my son, is what he'd tell you. Harvest is coming, I ought to be out there.

**W2 — Interviewer:** How did the surgery come about, and how have things gone since coming home?

**Hal:** Belly got hard as a rock and I couldn't keep anything down. June drove me to the hospital in town, the little one, and they sent me on to the big one. Fifty-five miles. They said it was a hernia had my gut twisted up, and they cut out a piece. Never been cut on before in my life.

**Interviewer:** And since coming home?

**Hal:** Fine. Sore. I told them I wanted to go home and they let me go on the fourth day.

**June (in background):** He *told* them. They didn't exactly offer.

**Hal:** Well, I was fine to go.

---

## Section B — Problem Discovery

**Q1 — Interviewer:** Walk me through the time since you left the hospital. What stands out, day and night?

**Hal:** It's only been four days. Sleep in the recliner because getting in and out of bed pulls on the stitches. Take the pills June sets out. Walk to the mailbox and back. That's about it.

**Interviewer:** What about nights?

**Hal:** Don't sleep much. Never have. Get up to use the bathroom two, three times. It's the same as it's always been, just slower.

**Interviewer:** Anything that's been harder than you expected?

**Hal:** Not eating much. Food doesn't taste like anything. They said that'd happen.

**Q2 — Interviewer:** Tell me about a time during recovery when something felt "off." What did you notice, what did you do, and how did it turn out?

**Hal:** Nothing's felt off. Sore, that's all.

*(pause)*

**June:** Tell him about yesterday.

**Hal:** There wasn't anything yesterday.

**June:** He didn't eat supper hardly at all, and he just didn't look right to me. Kind of gray. Kind of tired-looking.

**Interviewer:** What happened next?

**Hal:** It's the pain pills. They make you feel like you been run over. I went to bed early and I was fine this morning.

**Interviewer:** How did you decide it was the pain pills?

**Hal:** 'Cause I'm on pain pills. *(pause)* Look, if I called every time I felt poorly, I'd be on the phone all day. And they'd just say come in, and that's an hour each way. June's not supposed to drive that far at night, her eyes.

**Interviewer:** Did either of you check anything, like temperature?

**June:** I took it with the old thermometer. It said ninety-nine-something. I don't know if that thing's any good anymore.

**Hal:** Ninety-nine's nothing.

**Interviewer:** What would it have taken for you to call someone?

**Hal:** If I was throwing up again. Or couldn't get up. Then I'd call the clinic in town, Doc Kessler's place. Probably wait till morning, though. No sense getting everybody up at night.

**Q3 — Interviewer:** What are you doing today to keep track of how recovery is going? Walk me through the tools, routines, or people involved.

**Hal:** I don't keep track of it. I'll know if I'm sick. Been knowing that for eighty-one years.

**Interviewer:** Anyone else involved?

**Hal:** June watches me like a hawk. She fills the pill box on Sundays. My son stops by after chores most days, he's ten miles off.

**Interviewer:** Any calls or check-ins from the hospital?

**Hal:** Some gal called the first day, asked if I'd had a bowel movement. *(pause)* That was about the extent of it. There's a paper sheet with numbers on it somewhere.

**June:** It's on the fridge.

**Interviewer:** And do you have any follow-up appointment?

**Hal:** Two weeks, back at the surgeon's. My son's got to take a morning off to drive me. I'd rather see Doc Kessler, honestly. He's known me thirty years. Those fellas at the big hospital, nice enough, but they don't know me from Adam.

**Q4 — Interviewer:** Tell me about a health device, app, or routine you were asked to use at home and stopped using, or never started. What happened?

**Hal:** The breathing machine. For sleeping.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about that.

**Hal:** The CPAP. Mask on your face, hose, noise. I tried it maybe a week. It's in the closet.

**Interviewer:** What made you put it away?

**Hal:** Couldn't sleep with it. Felt like a horse with a feed bag on. And I slept fine seventy-some years without it.

**June:** He snores something awful without it.

**Hal:** She's managed.

**Interviewer:** Anything else like that?

**Hal:** They put a heart monitor on me a couple years back, for the fibrillation. Wires stuck on my chest. Supposed to wear it a couple days. I took it off the second morning. Itched like the dickens, and I had to get the grain moved.

**Interviewer:** What happened after you took it off?

**Hal:** Nothing. They put me on the blood thinner anyhow. So I don't know what the point of it was.

**Interviewer:** Anything on the phone, apps, anything like that?

**Hal:** I got a flip phone. My grandson set it up. I can call and answer. That's all it does as far as I'm concerned. And it don't work in the house half the time anyway, you got to stand out by the machine shed.

**Q5 — Interviewer:** Describe what your body, especially your hands, arms, and sleep, has been like since surgery. What have you been able or unable to wear or keep on comfortably?

**Hal:** Hands are the same as always. Big and stiff. Arthritis in the knuckles. They puffed up some in the hospital with all the IV fluid, seem better now.

**Interviewer:** What do you usually wear, day to day?

**Hal:** Overalls, when I'm not in these pajama pants. Watch — I quit wearing a watch years ago. Got caught on things. I don't even wear my wedding ring anymore. Swells up on the tractor, finger gets fat, I took it off before it cut the blood off. It's in June's jewelry box.

**June:** Twenty years now.

**Interviewer:** And the stitches, the belly — anything you couldn't keep on?

**Hal:** Waistband. Can't stand anything on the belly. That's why the pajama pants.

**Interviewer:** And sleep?

**Hal:** Already told you. Recliner, up and down all night. Bad sleep's normal for me.

**Q6 — Interviewer:** Tell me about the last time a reading, warning, or alarm turned out to be nothing. How did you feel, and what did you do the next time it went off?

**Hal:** The smoke alarm. Goes off every time June fries bacon.

**Interviewer:** What did you do about it?

**Hal:** Took the battery out.

**June:** He did. I put it back in.

**Hal:** And the combine, the newer one my son's got, beeps at you for everything. Half of it doesn't mean anything. You learn to tune it out.

**Interviewer:** Anything health-related, like that?

**Hal:** In the hospital, the machines beeping all night. Nurses didn't even come half the time. I figured if they weren't worried, I wasn't.

**Q7 — Interviewer:** How do you usually decide whether a health product or service is worth paying for, and who normally pays?

**Hal:** Medicare pays, and the supplement. If they don't pay, I don't need it.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the last time something wasn't covered.

**Hal:** Hearing aids. Wanted four thousand dollars. I said no thank you.

**June:** He can't hear me half the time.

**Hal:** I hear what I need to. *(pause)* We're on Social Security and a little from the co-op. I don't spend money on stuff I don't need. Never have.

**Interviewer:** Who in the house decides on things like that?

**Hal:** I do. June's got opinions. My son's got opinions. But I do.

**Q8 — Interviewer:** If you could change one thing about the time since you left the hospital, what would it be, and why that?

**Hal:** Wouldn't have had to go that far. If they could've done it in town, I'd have been home in a day and nobody driving back and forth.

**Interviewer:** Why that, over anything else?

**Hal:** Distance is everything out here. You get a question, it's an hour drive for an answer. So you don't ask. Nearest emergency room is twenty-five miles, and that's the little one. And if it's anything real they ship you to the city anyway.

**Interviewer:** Anything else?

**Hal:** *(long pause)* Just want to get back to normal. Coffee shop. Help with harvest. Not be sitting here being looked after.

## Section C — Neutral Concept Exposure

**Interviewer (reading verbatim):** "Some teams are exploring a small ring worn on the finger that passively measures heart rate, temperature, and similar signals around the clock for about two weeks after discharge. It learns a person's own normal pattern and can notify a care team if the pattern changes. It is an early idea, and I'm looking for honest reactions, including reasons it would not work."

**Q9 — Interviewer:** What's your first reaction? What, if anything, concerns you about it?

**Hal:** *(pause)* A ring. Well. I just told you I don't wear a ring.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more.

**Hal:** My fingers swell. Arthritis, the knuckles. I couldn't get a ring over them if I wanted to. And if it did get on, I'd be worried about getting it off.

**Interviewer:** What else concerns you?

**Hal:** Who's watching it? Somebody in the city looking at my pulse? The government doesn't need to know my pulse. *(pause)* And I don't need a gadget to tell me I'm sick. I'll know.

**June:** You didn't know yesterday.

**Hal:** There wasn't anything to know yesterday.

**Interviewer:** June, what's your first reaction?

**June:** I'd like somebody else keeping an eye, I'll be honest. I'm not a nurse. But I don't see him wearing it.

**Q10 — Interviewer:** Walk me through how something like this would fit, or not fit, into a normal day and night for you over those two weeks.

**Hal:** It wouldn't. Does it need plugged in?

**Interviewer:** Assume it would need charging every few days.

**Hal:** Then it'd be dead in a drawer. I can't keep track of charging the phone. June does that. *(pause)* And how's it tell anybody anything? Through the phone? My phone doesn't work in the house.

**Interviewer:** What about through a landline or home internet?

**Hal:** We don't have the internet. My son does. We got the landline.

**Interviewer:** Walk me through the first day if someone handed you one at discharge.

**Hal:** I'd probably put it on to be polite. Take it off when I washed up, or when it pinched. Then I'd forget where I put it. That's the honest answer. Same as the chest thing.

**Interviewer:** Is there any situation where you'd keep it on?

**Hal:** If Doc Kessler told me to. Maybe. Or if my grandson came over and made a fuss about it. He's good with that stuff. But the city surgeon? No.

**Q11 — Interviewer:** What would need to be true for you to trust a notification from something like this? What would make you stop paying attention to it?

**Hal:** I don't know that I would trust it. How does a ring know I'm sick? I've had a fever and gone out and baled hay.

**Interviewer:** What would make you pay attention if it said something?

**Hal:** If it was a real person calling. Somebody I know. Doc Kessler's nurse, Barb. Not a machine.

**Interviewer:** And what would make you stop paying attention?

**Hal:** First time it's wrong. Same as the smoke alarm. It calls and says drive an hour and I get there and it's nothing, that's the last time. Gas isn't free, and my son's got work.

**Interviewer:** If someone called and said your pattern had changed, what would you do?

**Hal:** Wait and see till morning, probably.

**June:** He would, too.

## Section D — Value & Willingness to Pay

**Q12 — Interviewer:** How would you expect something like this to be paid for? For the two-week period: at what price would it seem so cheap you'd doubt it works, a good deal, getting expensive, and too expensive to consider?

**Hal:** Medicare or nobody.

**Interviewer:** Let's take the question on its own. What price would seem so cheap you'd doubt it works?

**Hal:** I wouldn't doubt it for being cheap. I'd doubt it anyway.

**Interviewer:** What would feel like a good deal, for two weeks?

**Hal:** Free. If the hospital wants me wearing it, the hospital can pay for it.

**Interviewer:** What would start to feel expensive?

**Hal:** Anything I got to write a check for. I'm not being difficult, son, I just don't pay for extras. I didn't pay for the hearing aids.

**Interviewer:** And too expensive to consider?

**Hal:** Same answer. Anything.

**June:** If Dr. Kessler said he really needed it... I don't know. We'd talk about it. But I don't think he'd do it.

**Hal:** I wouldn't.

**Interviewer:** And if it were fully covered and cost you nothing?

**Hal:** Then I'd still have to wear the thing. Money's not the only cost.

---

## Section E — Close

**C1 — Interviewer:** Is there anything I should have asked but didn't?

**Hal:** You didn't ask how far it is. Everybody in the city forgets that. You can have all the gadgets you want. If something's wrong, it's still an hour to the surgeon and twenty-five minutes to the little ER. And the phones don't work out here.

**Interviewer:** Anything else?

**Hal:** No. That's plenty.

**C2 — Interviewer:** Who else should I talk to about this?

**Hal:** Doc Kessler, in town. If he thought it was worth anything, folks around here would listen. They won't listen to you. No offense.

**June:** Our grandson, maybe. Tyler. He does all the phone things for us.

**Hal:** And talk to the fellas at the coffee shop, if you want to hear what people really think. You'll get an earful.

**Interviewer:** Thank you both for your time. One last thing, not part of the study: June mentioned yesterday he didn't look right and had a temperature around 99. The number on the fridge is there for exactly that, and it's worth a call to them or to Dr. Kessler's clinic if you're unsure.

**Hal:** We'll see how I feel tomorrow.

**June:** I'll call Barb.
