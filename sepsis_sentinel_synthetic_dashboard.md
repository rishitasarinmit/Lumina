# The Sepsis Sentinel: Synthetic Customer-Discovery Dashboard Report

> **MANDATORY DISCLAIMER (SYNTHETIC RESEARCH):** Synthetic user research provides a rapid, low-cost hypothesis test, but IT CANNOT RELIABLY PREDICT REAL HUMAN BEHAVIOR. All findings, objections, and willingness-to-pay metrics generated by synthetic personas must be validated through primary market research with real patients, clinicians, and health system buyers.

## 1. Executive summary

- The 14-day post-discharge window has a real, severe unmet need, but the need is for an accountable human interpreter, especially at night, rather than for a device. 8/10 personas asked for an accountable human owner or interpreter when asked what one thing they would change.
- A1 (adherence) is invalidated as stated: 0 Validated, 5 Partial, 5 Invalidated. All 10 personas raised ring fit, and the patients at highest risk are least able to wear a ring.
- A2 (workflow/alert fatigue) is invalidated as stated: 0 Validated, 5 Partial, 5 Invalidated, with 3/4 clinical/buyers invalidating it. No hub covers nights, and trust collapses after 1-3 false alarms. The viable form is a vendor-staffed 24/7 triage layer.
- A3 (RPM codes to avoid readmission penalties) is invalidated as stated: 0 Validated, 6 Partial, 4 Invalidated. Money exists in TEAM episode budgets, ACO lives, integrated plans and risk-share. RPM is a partial offset at best, and sepsis is not an HRRP condition.
- A4 (patient pull) is partially validated: 2 Validated, 5 Partial, 3 Invalidated. Anxiety is near-universal, but pull is for a person. Passive tracking is welcome when it is prescribed by the patient's own clinician and serves a named human.
- B2B WTP median good value is $125/episode (stated range $100-250, n=3). Buyers expect it to include 24/7 staffed triage. B2C out-of-pocket is not a viable channel: 3/6 patients/caregivers (50%) refuse any out-of-pocket payment.
- Pivot hypothesis: sell a surgeon-prescribed, vendor-staffed post-discharge triage service for TEAM episodes, with an edema-tolerant wearable (ring or alternative) as the sensor. Price it per episode with a risk-share component. The Persona is the RPM nurse manager.

### 3.5 Reimbursement reality notes (verify before use)

These notes are context for reading the synthetic answers. They are not legal or billing advice. Verify current CMS rules and payer policy with revenue-cycle and compliance experts.

- **HRRP does not target sepsis.** The Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program measures AMI, heart failure, pneumonia, COPD, CABG and elective THA/TKA, and the penalty is capped at 3% of base DRG payments. Post-surgical sepsis is not a target condition in its own right. A readmission after an HRRP index procedure (CABG, THA/TKA) may still count toward that procedure's measure; verify with the current measure specifications.
- **RPM codes fit a 14-day episode poorly.** Device-supply code 99454 historically required at least 16 days of data in a 30-day period. CMS's CY2026 Physician Fee Schedule added shorter-duration options (a 2-15 day device-supply code and a 10-minute management code). Verify current CMS rules and payer policy before modelling RPM revenue (99453 setup, 99454 device supply, 99457/99458 management time).
- **Global surgical period.** Billing RPM inside a surgical global period is an open compliance question that needs validation with revenue-cycle and compliance staff.
- **CMS TEAM is where buyers say the money lands.** The Transforming Episode Accountability Model is a mandatory episode model that started in January 2026 for select surgical episodes (including LEJR, CABG, major bowel procedures, spinal fusion and hip/femur fracture) at selected hospitals. Episode budgets include 30 days after discharge, so avoided readmissions and avoided ED visits flow to the episode.
- **Patient cost-sharing.** RPM services billed under Medicare Part B may carry patient coinsurance (verify). Any cost-sharing is a dealbreaker for P5, P9 and P10.

### 5.5 Surprising and counterintuitive insights

1. Every patient/caregiver (6/6) had a sub-threshold low-grade temperature episode (99.x to 100.2°F) in the first days home, and none met the call-above-101°F instruction. All 4 night-time episodes (P1, P6, P9, P10) waited until morning or never called. The single-threshold discharge sheet is the real incumbent, and it produces false reassurance.
2. The Innovator is not the easy customer. P1 already owns an Oura, refuses a second ring, and would adopt a software/API layer on his existing wearables 'tomorrow'. The most enthusiastic segment wants the service, not the hardware.
3. The economic buyer's all-in price including vendor overnight monitoring (P8: $100-150 good value) is lower than the champion's device-only price (P2: $150-250). The CFO discounts the champion by roughly 40% while also expecting more to be included.
4. The same ring form factor helps and hurts. Rosa (P3) wears her late husband's ring every day ('not against jewelry'), while arthritic/RA hands (P9, P10, P6's husband) make any ring impossible, and those patients are among the oldest and highest-risk.
5. Payer or free funding can reduce trust. P10: 'If it's free, I'm the product', and he asks why the insurer is paying. P9: 'Money's not the only cost.' Zero price does not produce adoption.
6. Continuous data can increase institutional liability rather than reduce risk. P2, P4 and P7 each independently said that data collected but not acted on becomes exposure (P7: 'Exhibit A'). P4's real case: vitals drifted all weekend in a closed hub.
7. Night is both where the most value is and where operations are weakest. It has the cleanest signal (P2), the most caregiver anxiety (P3, P6), and zero hub coverage (P2, P4, P8).
8. The price of the device is dominated by labor. P4: 'A cheap device that doubles my alert volume is the most expensive thing you could sell me.' P2: the threshold 'isn't a technical decision, it's a staffing decision'. Buyers price by alerts per 100 patients, not per ring.
9. Under fee-for-service, avoided readmissions can reduce hospital revenue (P8), and false-alarm ED visits can worsen TEAM/ACO performance. The product has a positive ROI only in risk-bearing contracts, which narrows the beachhead to TEAM episodes and integrated/ACO lives.
10. The immunocompromised patient (P5) wants the data mainly as credibility at the ED door ('That somebody believes me the first time'). This patient-advocacy use case, such as a call-ahead to triage, is absent from the concept.
11. Messenger beats device. At least 6 personas said the wearer would comply only if their own trusted clinician prescribed it (P1 surgeon enrolling, P3 surgeon in Spanish, P5 transplant coordinator, P6 David's doctor, P9 family doctor Doc Kessler, P10 own surgeon face-to-face), and the trusted messenger differs by persona.
12. Patients disagree about data visibility. P1 and P5 insist on seeing their own raw numbers, while P3 and P6 explicitly do not want more numbers or a 2 a.m. app, and want a clinician to filter first.

## 7. Key insights and implications by Disciplined Entrepreneurship step

### DE Step 3: End User Profile

Narrow the End User Profile from all adults 60+ discharged after any inpatient surgery to elective CMS TEAM episodes (major bowel, CABG, lower-extremity joint, spinal fusion) at health systems in TEAM that have an RPM hub and/or an integrated health plan or ACO lives. Treat the end user as a triad: (a) the wearer (older, swollen/arthritic hands, frequently on opioids), (b) the family caregiver who actually operates the device and receives the calls (P2: caregivers are 'the real operators'), and (c) the RPM/triage nurse who consumes alerts. Elective surgery provides a pre-op baseline window (P1, P7). Immunocompromised patients have high need because of blunted fever (P5). Deprioritize or design alternatives for emergency surgeries (no baseline, e.g. P9), patients with RA or severe arthritis, and patients without cellular coverage.

### DE Step 5: Persona (recommendation)

Recommend THE Persona be Karen Mitchell, RN (RPM Nurse Manager). The value can only be realized through her team, and she holds the practical veto: P2 said the RPM manager 'has veto power in practice'. Every buyer priced the product through nurse workload, and A2 (her domain) was the most clearly invalidated assumption among clinical/buyers (3/4). Design the wearer experience for Rosa/Maria (P3): an older patient who is not a gadget user, plus an engaged caregiver who is the realistic early-adopter dyad. Keep Okafor (champion) and Halvorsen (economic buyer) for the DMU (Step 12). Do not make Raj (P1) the persona: he is unrepresentative, already owns an Oura, and rejects a second ring.

### DE Step 6: Full Life Cycle Use Case

Full Life Cycle Use Case, revised: (1) Surgeon's office enrolls the patient at the pre-op visit through an Epic order; (2) ring sizing kit at pre-admission testing, with a 7-14 day pre-op baseline for elective cases; (3) removed for the OR, and a larger size or an alternative form factor (wrist/patch) is issued at discharge if fingers are swollen; (4) cellular hub, no phone pairing; teach-back to the caregiver in the patient's language; (5) days 0-14: vendor-staffed 24/7 nurse triage watches post-op-trajectory-adjusted deviations; non-wear or low battery triggers a caregiver nudge, never a clinical alert; (6) tiered escalation: nurse call → photo/recheck → same-day visit/CRP → ED with call-ahead; Epic In Basket only for pre-triaged escalations; (7) structured human touchpoints on about day 2, 5 and 9 (P4, P7, P10); (8) day 14: prepaid return mailer, data deletion confirmation, outcome report to service line and finance.

### DE Step 7: High-Level Product Specification

High-Level Product Spec: the product is a service (24/7 triage and escalation protocol) with a wearable attached, not a ring with a dashboard. Must-haves: multi-size or adjustable fit tolerant of edema and arthritis, plus a non-ring alternative; at least 7-day battery or charge-while-worn, so there is no nightly removal; cellular hub instead of smartphone Bluetooth; non-wear/signal-loss detection kept separate from clinical alerts; explainable alerts (which signal, magnitude, window, versus a population post-op trajectory plus a personal pre-op baseline); medication and comorbidity context (immunosuppression, beta-blockers, diabetes); Epic integration with no standalone login; bias-audited PPG across skin tones; multilingual caregiver notifications (Spanish, Chinese); patient data export (P1, P5); strict no-secondary-use/deletion terms. Consider an Oura/Apple Health BYOD integration tier (P1).

### DE Step 8: Quantified Value Proposition

Quantified Value Proposition in the buyer's currency: (a) alerts per 100 patient-days and PPV, with a target of less than one-third false positives forwarded to health-system staff; (b) nurse minutes per enrolled patient, with net-zero or negative time by displacing post-discharge calls or the call vendor (P8: 2 FTE at ~$130K loaded plus a low-six-figure vendor); (c) lead time in hours versus current detection; (d) avoided TEAM-episode readmissions at ~$15-25K each. P8's break-even is ~15-25 avoided per ~1,100 episodes at $200/episode plus 1 FTE, and he requires 1.5x. For caregivers: 'somebody knows before 7 a.m.', measured as caregiver sleep hours and anxiety scores.

### DE Steps 16/17: Pricing Framework and LTV

Pricing Framework: B2B per-episode, all-in (device, logistics, 24/7 triage, Epic integration), anchored at $125-200 with an outcomes-based or risk-share component on documented TEAM episode savings (P8's preferred structure). Avoid a device-only SKU below ~$50-75, which reads as 'a consumer ring with a dashboard bolted on'. Do not rely on B2C: median out-of-pocket good deal is ~$39 per two weeks and half refuse, and patient coinsurance on RPM codes (verify) is a barrier for fixed-income patients. Treat RPM codes as an upside offset. LTV (illustrative only): a mid-size system with ~1,000-1,200 TEAM/ACO episodes/yr at $125-200 is ~$125-240K ARR, expanding across service lines. Unit-economics risk: one 24/7 triage seat needs ~4.5-5 FTE (~$600K+/yr at P8's loaded cost), so triage must be pooled across many systems before the price covers labor.

## 9. Limitations and methodology

### 9.1 Methodology

- Cell scoring rule: Per cell: Validated = persona's own words support the assumption as stated without material conditions; Partially Validated = supports it only under explicit conditions or with mixed/indirect evidence; Invalidated = persona's words contradict it as stated. Verdict: Validated only if a majority validate with no invalidation majority in the decisive segment; Invalidated if no persona validates it as stated and >=50% of the decisive segment (patients/caregivers for A1 and A4; clinical/buyers for A2 and A3) invalidate; otherwise Partially Validated.
- Indirect evidence: clinicians/buyers are scored on A1 (adherence) and A4 (patient pull) by what they report about patients. Patients/caregivers are scored on A3 by whether their answers support the specific RPM-codes/readmission-penalty thesis.
- Coding: a single analyst scored every cell and coded objections into 15 themes (one count per persona per theme). No second coder or inter-rater check was run.
- Van Westendorp: numbers as stated; ranges stored as midpoints; B2C and B2B kept separate; refusals flagged.
- Quotes: every quoted string was programmatically checked as an exact substring of the named persona's transcript.

### 9.2 Limitations

- Synthetic personas cannot predict real behavior. Every score, objection and price here is an LLM's plausible simulation, not evidence. Treat all findings as hypotheses to prioritize real research, not as validation.
- Persona design choices shaped the results. The 10 personas were deliberately spread across the adoption curve, with specific barriers written into their sheets (e.g., P9's swollen ring finger, P10's RA and privacy fears, P5's skin-tone concern, P8's HRRP skepticism). Many objections are therefore partly the persona brief speaking back. The mix (1 Innovator, 2 Laggard/Rejector patients) is not a representative sample of the beachhead.
- LLM homogeneity risk. The transcripts share a voice: articulate, well-organized, well-informed about 2026 CMS policy, and prone to converge on the same socially desirable answer (a person over a device). Real interviewees are more varied, less coherent, and less informed. Recurring phrases may reflect model style rather than real shared vernacular.
- Interviewer and concept priming. Q2 introduced the word 'off', and the concept statement introduced 'learns a person's own normal' and 'care team'. Recurrence of these phrases is partly an artifact.
- n = 10 (6 patients/caregivers, 4 clinical/buyers; B2B WTP n = 3). Counts are directional only, with no statistical power. Medians of 3 numbers are not a price.
- Timing gap. Patients were interviewed on days 3-6 after discharge, so days 7-14, the classic leak/SSI window, are unobserved.
- Scoring subjectivity. Assumption scores were assigned by a single analyst using the rubric above. Clinician/buyer A1 and A4 scores and patient A3 scores rest on indirect evidence. A second coder and real-data inter-rater checks are needed.
- Reimbursement and policy statements (RPM short-duration codes, TEAM scope, HRRP penalties, global period) are claims made by synthetic personas and must be verified with real revenue-cycle and compliance experts.
